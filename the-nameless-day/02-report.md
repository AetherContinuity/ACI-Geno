# Part Two: The Report

**BANA – Agency for Banana Economy and Investment Assessment**
Inspection Report 1/IX
Subject: Greenhouse 3, Row 14 – loss of Bunch 1/2033
Recorded by: K.

## Entry

The entry that follows the empty evening column in the Dock Book is dated the way the Association dates everything, by the day it actually was:

| Date | Trucks | Bananas | Remarks |
|---|--:|--:|---|
| 8.11.2034 | 0 | 142 | Bunch. 0 edible. |

By the state calendar it was the first day of the Month of Promise, Year IX, an Initiative Day. The Month of Promise carries the official meaning *everything is possible*. That morning, it no longer held.

The bunch was found in Greenhouse 3, Row 14, at 6.14 a.m., by the same member of the Association who had seen it flower. He did not touch it. He walked back to the loading dock and wrote it down.

The bunch hung from its stalk, brown and soft. The skins were whole. There were no bite marks. No one had eaten any of it. BANA classified the cause of loss as *natural*. In the Bananna State this was unheard of.

The Association reported the death to BANA, because BANA's number was the only one printed in the Association's rules. BANA informed the Ministry of Finance. The Ministry informed the insurer. The insurer informed the pledgee. The pledgee was the Kingdom of Sweden, and the Kingdom of Sweden sent a customs inspector.

---

Customs Inspector A. Strömberg of the Haparanda customs office arrived by train the next day, the second day of the Month of Promise, a Preparation Day. He carried a briefcase, a thermos, and a copy of the Dock Book, which Swedish Customs had bought three years earlier for three kilos of lingonberry jam.

He took a room at the Puijo lodge, an old timber house on the wooded hill above Kuopio, with the observation tower on one side and the glass dome on the other. Two guests had arrived on the same train: V. and the undersigned. The Ministry of the Future had commissioned us to assess the harvesting protocol for the bunch and had not cancelled the commission. The harvesting protocol was no longer needed.

Strömberg shook our hands and addressed me as Director-General. I corrected him. He did not record the correction.

He asked for every document concerning the bunch. The next morning he received 1,200 pages in fourteen binders. He read them in two days at the kitchen table, with the thermos at his elbow. He was the first person to read all of them. No one had expected this, and no one had prepared for it.

Binder 9 contained a printout from the access control system of Greenhouse 3, Airlock 3. Strömberg turned its pages quickly, because they were lists of times and names. On the last page was a line with a time and no date:

| Date | Time | Event |
|---|---|---|
| | 18:15:00 | DATE INVALID. ACCESS PENDING. |

I recorded the binder as received. I did not record the line, because it was a line in a document already received.

---

On the evening of the second day Strömberg laid two papers side by side on the table. One was a page from the Dock Book. The other was BANA's flowering notice, with the archive index stamp in its upper corner.

"According to the Dock Book, the flowering was observed on the twenty-second of April 2033," he said. "According to BANA, on the seventeenth of June 2033."

"Yes," I said.

"Two observations."

He wrote it in his notebook: *Two observations.* He looked at the two dates for a while. Then he put the Dock Book page aside, because it was not an official document, and took a third paper, the ripening forecast, which gave the second of January 2035.

"The bunch died fifty-five days before it would have been ripe," he said. "A banana does not rot before it ripens."

He looked at me, and then at V.

"Someone did this."

V. said nothing. I recorded it.

---

On the third morning Strömberg wanted to see Row 14. The airlock display showed the time and the date: the third day of the Month of Promise, Coordination Day. The door opened for him without delay. He did not look at the display, because there was nothing on it to look at.

On the way back he stopped at the north gate of the dome.

On the ground beside the glass wall lay a pair of sunglasses, a scarf and a carrot. Beneath them was a frozen puddle. It reached all the way to the glass, and hardly at all in the other direction.

"A local custom?" he asked.

"A snowman," I said.

"Where is he?"

"He was here."

Strömberg looked at the puddle for a moment and walked on. He wrote nothing in his notebook. I recorded the puddle, because this is a BANA inspection report, and BANA records everything.

---

That evening Strömberg asked whether the statement Sweden had sent as pledgee had arrived. It had been posted on the twenty-seventh of October. No one knew. He checked BANA's registry. Against the statement it said: *Sent by Bananna State Post, 25 B commemorative stamp. Received: no.*

"Snail mail," he said, to no one.

---

At dusk he walked out to Loading Dock 4. The capuchins were sitting there, as they had sat every morning since the flowering. It was evening now, and they had not left.

"Who did this?" Strömberg asked.

The Association's eldest member answered. He had escaped from the dome in its first summer, as the environmental impact assessment had said someone would.

"We counted."

"What did you count?"

"Whose it was."

"And whose was it?"

"The eater's."

Strömberg waited. No one went on.

"Who ate it?"

"No one," said the eldest. "That is why you are here."

---

That night Strömberg wrote the first line in his notebook that was not a quotation from a document:

*Sabotage. Motive: ?*

He was wrong. I know that now. I did not know it then, and I recorded it as he said it.

## The Night Dock

*Recorder's note, the night between the fourth and fifth days of the Month of Promise (11–12.11.2034)*

Strömberg was reading the insurance contract at the kitchen table. It was eleven o'clock. He had asked the insurer's local representative for the contract that evening, and the representative had delivered it promptly. No one had ever before asked him for a contract in order to read it.

The contract ran to nine pages. On the eighth, Strömberg stopped and read aloud:

> Should the work in progress fail to be completed before the forecast completion date (2.1.2035), the insurer shall compensate the assessed value of the work, 2,400,000,000 Banana Units.

He put the paper down.

"A living bunch is worth one bunch," he said. "A dead bunch is worth the national debt."

He was not speaking to me. He underlined the name of the beneficiary. I did not see the name, because he turned the page.

I recorded: *Strömberg has found a motive.*

---

At half past eleven I went outside. I could not sleep. V. followed, as he does when there is no reason to stay in.

We walked to Loading Dock 4. It was empty. The capuchins were in the condensate pipe, because it was night and twelve degrees below zero. For the first time in a year and a half, no one was sitting on the dock.

We sat on the edge. The boards were cold. The data centre's exhaust duct ran beneath the dock, and its warmth reached our ankles but not the wood.

I have built a dock. I know that a dock is finished only when someone can step onto it. This dock had been finished since 2026. In a year and a half it had been stepped on by one driver, 412 trucks and the Association. Of bananas, one peel had passed over it.

V. looked at the dome. I looked in the same direction.

---

At 0.20 a vehicle approached from the direction of Puijo. It was a Bananna State Post delivery van. It drove slowly, because in the Bananna State postal vans always drive slowly, and stopped at the end of the dock.

The driver got out with a letter in his hand and read the address under the lamp.

"Greenhouse three, row fourteen," he read.

"The row is inside the dome," I said.

"Can post go in there?"

"No."

He handed the letter to me, because I was nearest, and asked for a signature. I signed. On the envelope was a 25 B commemorative stamp: a laurel-crowned man holding a banana toward the sun. The postmark was 27.10.2034. The sender was Swedish Customs, on behalf of the pledgee.

The letter had been in transit for sixteen days. From Haparanda to Kuopio is 420 kilometres. The letter had travelled 26 kilometres a day.

The driver drove away slowly.

I recorded: *Received: statement of the Swedish pledgee, addressed to the bunch. Addressee deceased.*

---

Before we went back, we walked to the north gate. The sunglasses and the scarf lay where they had lain, on top of the puddle, and the puddle had frozen hard. The carrot was gone.

I looked around in the lamplight. In the snow there were small tracks leading toward the condensate pipe. They were four-footed, and beside them ran a thin line, as if a tail had been dragged.

I recorded: *Evidence removed from the scene.*

V. bent down and pressed his palm against the glass of the dome, where the puddle met it. He held it there for a while. Then he looked at me.

I pressed my own palm against the glass beside his. The glass was warm, on the outside, at twelve degrees below zero.

I recorded: *Glass warm. Dome heating operating as planned.*

---

At 1.05 we returned to the lodge. Strömberg was still at the kitchen table with the contract. I gave him the letter.

He opened it and read it. It was one page. He read it twice, folded it back into the envelope and put the envelope in his breast pocket.

"Routine," he said. "The pledgee's statement. It no longer matters."

"What does it say?"

"That the pledgee does not object."

"To what?"

He looked at me as if the question were unnecessary.

"To the harvest."

Then he went back to page eight.

I recorded: *Content of statement: routine (Strömberg).* He did not ask me to enter the statement in the registry, and I did not.

---

I recorded the events of the night in order:

1. Strömberg found a motive.
2. The Swedish statement arrived sixteen days late.
3. The carrot disappeared.
4. The glass of the dome was warm.

None of them concerned the insurance.

It was 1.40 when I went to bed. By the state calendar, the fifth day of the Month of Promise had begun. It was Consultation Day.

---

## In Three Calendars

*Recorder's note, the fifth and sixth days of the Month of Promise (12–13.11.2034)*

Strömberg spread three sheets of paper across the kitchen table and taped them together. With a ruler he drew a timeline from the flowering to the death of the bunch. At the flowering he wrote BANA's date, 17.6.2033, and at the end 8.11.2034. The forecast ripening date, 2.1.2035, he marked in red off the end of the timeline, on the edge of the table, where there was no room for it.

"The bunch never got this far," he said, and tapped the red mark.

Then he asked for the minutes of every body that had dealt with the bunch between the twenty-seventh of October and the seventh of November. He received eleven binders that afternoon. Binder 11 also contained a letter he had not asked for: the seventh polite inquiry from the harbour master at Narvik, asking when the first banana shipment of the Bananna State's Silk Road might be expected. It had been filed under *Silk Road, pending*. Strömberg set it aside.

I recorded: *Binder 11 contains an irrelevant document.*

---

Strömberg went through the meetings day by day and marked each on the timeline. In the state calendar every day has a name, and on every named day a meeting had been held. On Consultation Day a statement had been requested. On Steering Group Day the steering group had met. On Interim Report Day an interim report had been prepared. On Impact Day BANA had assessed impact. On Follow-on Funding Day follow-on funding had been sought.

On the additional days there had been festivities, and every festivity had minutes.

"Everyone has an alibi," Strömberg said. "Every body, for every day. Everyone was in a meeting."

He leaned back.

"So the culprit needed no hands. The weapon is the contract."

He showed me page eight of the insurance contract. The name of the beneficiary was underlined. Now I saw it: *Ministry of Finance.*

"If the bunch dies before the second of January, the Ministry receives the national debt. No one has to do anything. It is enough that no one does anything. The contract does the rest."

I recorded: *The calendars support Strömberg's theory.*

V. looked at the timeline for a long time. His gaze stopped at the point where the additional days ended and Year IX began. On the timeline the gap was one day wide. Strömberg had left it empty.

---

On the morning of the sixth day of the Month of Promise, Steering Group Day, Strömberg wanted to hear the observer from the Food and Agriculture Organization. He had found the name in binder 4, on the last line of a distribution list: *Paredes, E. (FAO), for information.*

Don Esteban Paredes lived in the service building of Greenhouse 3, because it was warm there. He had been in Kuopio since 2031. He came from Ecuador, from the province of El Oro, and he had grown bananas for forty years. In his room there was a bed, a kettle and a wall calendar that was neither of the Bananna State's calendars. It contained only numbers: days since flowering.

He spoke English slowly and precisely.

"I am here since 2031," he said. "Nobody asks me. You are the first."

"What is your task?" Strömberg asked.

"To observe. I observe. I write reports. Nobody reads."

"Did you see the bunch?"

"Every day."

"When did it ripen?"

Paredes looked at his wall calendar. When he answered, he did not speak English. He spoke the way a man speaks when he is counting in his head in his own language.

"La flor salió el veintidós de abril. El racimo estaba listo el veintisiete. Doce días para comerlo. Nadie vino."

Strömberg waited for a translation. Paredes did not translate, because he considered the matter clear.

I recorded: *Witness answered in Spanish. Translation missing. BANA has no Spanish translator. Translation commissioned from the Ministry of the Future.*

"Do you understand?" Strömberg asked me.

"No."

He turned to V. V. was looking at Paredes. He said nothing.

---

Before we left, Strömberg asked one more question, perhaps out of courtesy.

"What do you think of the Bananna State?"

Paredes smiled for the first time.

"O. Henry wrote 'banana republic' about Honduras. Nineteen-oh-four. A real banana republic has bananas. It has too many bananas, and the wrong people eat them."

He looked out of the window at Greenhouse 3.

"Here, nobody eats. This is something else. I don't have the word."

I recorded: *Witness does not have the word.*

---

In the afternoon Strömberg added a fourth sheet to the timeline. On it he set out three calendars side by side, as I had done on the page of the Dock Book:

| | State calendar | Association calendar | Paredes's calendar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flowering | 17.6. VII (BANA conversion 17.6.2033) | the day of the flower | *el veintidós de abril* |
| Ripening | forecast 2.1.2035 | "when the snowman began to weep" | *el veintisiete* |
| Edible | – | – | *doce días* |
| Death | Month of Promise 1, IX | 8.11. 0 edible. | *nadie vino* |

*Recorder's note:* Of the three calendars only one is official. The other two are recorded for completeness.

Strömberg looked at the table and nodded.

"Three calendars, one truth," he said. "The bunch died before it ripened. The contract pays. The Ministry benefits."

That evening he called Haparanda and asked for authority to hear the Ministry of Finance formally. Authority was granted.

After the call he took the pledgee's statement from his breast pocket, looked at the envelope and put it back unopened.

V. went to bed early. Before he did, he stopped at the table and touched the empty place on the timeline with his forefinger, the day between Year VIII and Year IX that had no name.

I recorded: *V. examined the timeline.*

---

## The Silent Witness

*Recorder's note, the seventh day of the Month of Promise, Interim Report Day (14.11.2034)*

Mr Hargreaves arrived from London in the morning, flew into Kuopio and took a taxi to the Puijo lodge. He wore a grey cardigan and rubber boots and carried a leather notebook older than the Bananna State. He represented the reinsurer that had assumed a risk of 2.4 billion Banana Units. He had come to find out why the risk had materialised.

He shook Strömberg's hand for a long time, the way two investigators shake hands when they know they are investigating the same thing for different reasons.

"I understand you have a theory," Hargreaves said.

"I have a motive," Strömberg said.

"Splendid. I have a weather condition."

I recorded: *There are now two investigators.*

---

Before lunch a third visitor came to the lodge and did not stay. Dr Haas of the International Atomic Energy Agency in Vienna had arrived under the Bananna State's observer partnership to inspect the small modular reactor. She had spent two days looking for it and had established that it did not exist.

She left a copy of her measurement record at the lodge, because the distribution list required it to be delivered to "all inspectors present". The record was one page with one graph: the surface temperature of the dome's glass from satellite thermal imagery over the past year. The line was flat until the twenty-seventh of October 2034, when it rose steeply and stayed up.

The conclusion read: *There is no nuclear plant on the site. The most significant heat source is the de-icing of the glass. The observation does not fall within the Agency's remit.*

At the bottom of the page, in small print, was a footnote: *Method: satellite thermal infrared channel, pixel size 100 m. Glass is opaque in the thermal infrared; the measurement therefore describes the temperature of the glass surface.*

Dr Haas left on the afternoon train. She did not ask about the bunch.

I recorded: *IAEA record filed. Does not concern nuclear power.*

Hargreaves asked for a copy and read it twice.

---

After lunch Strömberg went looking for the frost man.

He had found him in binder 6: photocopies of a field notebook with a blue cover, kept by A. Hämäläinen, junior frost-maintenance officer, every page stamped *RECEIVED. FILED.* Strömberg had not read the figures. He had read one remark, from the thirteenth of October: *Snowman at point 17, against the glass. Sunglasses. Not mine.* He wanted to ask whose they were.

The Frost Maintenance Office gave him an address in Neulamäki, a wooden house on a slope on the south side of the city, with a large window facing north. From the window you could see Puijo, and on top of Puijo the faint amber glow that the city no longer noticed.

An old man opened the door. He was in his seventies and stood carefully, as people stand whose knees have been finished for some time.

"I am looking for A. Hämäläinen," Strömberg said. "Of the Frost Maintenance Office."

The old man did not answer. He let us into the hall, because it was cold.

"Is he at home?"

The old man said nothing. He went to the window and looked out toward Puijo. High on the window frame, above the old man's head, someone had once written a word in pencil: *ammunition*.

"I would like to ask him about his measurements," Strömberg said. "Point 17. The north gate."

The old man stood at the window for a while longer. Then he turned away from it and went into the kitchen, and we heard the tap run.

Strömberg waited. He left his card on the hall table, under the edge of a plant pot, so that it would not blow away when the door opened.

I recorded: *Visit to the frost-maintenance officer's address. Officer not at home. Father present. No answer received. Card left.*

---

In the afternoon both investigators wished to hear the snowman.

Nothing remained of the snowman but the sunglasses, the scarf and the frozen puddle. Both men, however, considered a hearing necessary, and both their procedures required one. The hearing was held at the north gate at 14.00. The undersigned kept the record.

*Hearing record 2/IX*

**Strömberg:** Were you at the north gate between the twenty-seventh of October and the seventh of November?

*The witness did not answer.*

**Hargreaves:** Did you notice any unusual warmth?

*The witness did not answer.*

**Strömberg:** Did you see anyone enter Greenhouse 3?

*The witness did not answer.*

**Hargreaves:** Did the glass feel warm to you?

*The witness did not answer.*

Hargreaves closed his notebook.

"The witness has answered," he said.

Strömberg looked at him.

"He said nothing."

"He melted," Hargreaves said. "That is an answer."

He knelt beside the puddle in his rubber boots and measured it with a pocket tape. The puddle was 140 centimetres long and reached the glass. Away from the glass it extended 11 centimetres.

"Asymmetric," he said. "The heat came from the glass, not from the sun. There is no sun here in November. This is a thermal event."

He took out the IAEA record and pointed to the rise in the graph.

"The twenty-seventh of October. The glass warmed suddenly. The snowman began to melt the same day. The bunch over-ripened in the same heat. No one caused this, and no one could have foreseen it. *Force majeure.* Under clause 11 of the contract, no compensation is payable."

Strömberg was silent. Then he said:

"According to BANA's forecast, the bunch would ripen in January. If the heat had been normal, the bunch would have lived until January. Someone raised the heat."

"Or," Hargreaves said politely, "the forecast was wrong."

Neither of them said anything for a while.

I recorded: *Two theories: sabotage and natural event.* I did not record Hargreaves's last sentence, because it was an aside.

---

When the hearing was over, the chair of the Association came to the north gate. He had followed the proceedings from the mouth of the condensate pipe.

Hargreaves turned to him.

"Evidence has gone missing from the scene. The snowman's nose."

"We ate it," said the chair.

"You ate the evidence."

"It existed."

Strömberg stepped forward.

"You ate the carrot, but not the bunch."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The chair looked at him as if the question were obvious.

"There was no paper for the carrot."

No one said anything.

I recorded the answer verbatim, because it was an answer to a question put at a hearing. In the recorder's note I wrote: *The Association's answer confirms that the bunch was not touched.*

---

When the others went back to the lodge, V. stayed at the north gate. He lifted the scarf from the puddle. It had frozen to the ice, and he freed it carefully so that it would not tear. He folded it twice and put it in his coat pocket. He left the sunglasses where they were.

I recorded: *Scarf removed from the scene (V.).* I added that the removal took place in the presence of the recorder and that no objection was made.

---

That evening the lodge held two investigators and two solutions. Strömberg sat at the kitchen table with his timeline, Hargreaves in the sitting room with the IAEA record. Both were right that the bunch was dead, and both were right that no one had touched it.

Strömberg said that someone had wanted the bunch to die. Hargreaves said that no one could have prevented it.

I recorded: *There are two solutions in the investigation. They are not the same solution.*

Before I went to bed I wrote one more line, in my own notebook, not in the report. I do not know why I wrote it. It was a sentence that neither investigator had said aloud:

~~*The heat was foreseeable. It was in the graph.*~~

I crossed it out, because it was not the recorder's task.

---

## Nameless Day

*Recorder's note, the eighth day of the Month of Promise, Impact Day (15.11.2034)*

In the morning the radio was talking about a hurricane.

It had been named Marisol, and it had come ashore on the north coast of Honduras two days earlier. The Sula valley was under water. According to the news, about two thirds of the country's banana plantations had been destroyed, and hundreds of people were dead. The number grew with every bulletin.

Storms are given their names in advance, from lists drawn up years before. No storm has ever had to wait for one.

Paredes listened to the news in the lodge kitchen, where he had come for morning coffee for the first time. His own plantations were in El Oro, in another country, on another coast. He called home anyway. The call was long, and he spoke quietly. Afterwards he sat at the table with the cup in his hands and did not drink.

I did not record the call. It did not concern the investigation.

---

At nine Hargreaves's phone rang. He listened standing up, said *yes* three times and *of course* once, and ended the call.

"I have been recalled," he said. "The reinsurer has losses in Honduras that are larger than this."

He packed his bag in ten minutes. At the door he shook Strömberg's hand for as long as he had on arriving.

"I shall send my *force majeure* opinion from the airport. You need not agree with it."

"I do not," Strömberg said.

Hargreaves nodded, as if that had been expected. Then he looked out of the window at Greenhouse 3.

"Something happened there," he said. "Nothing happened here."

The taxi took him to the airport.

I recorded: *Hargreaves has left. There is one investigator.*

---

At ten Strömberg called Haparanda to report. The call was short. When it ended he sat for a moment without speaking.

"The world market price of bananas has risen forty per cent in two days," he said. "The book value of the pledge has risen in the same proportion. Haparanda is pleased."

"The bunch is rotten," I said.

"The pledge is not the bunch," he said. "The pledge is paper."

He did not say it with pleasure.

---

He counted the rows. Twelve.

Then he counted the Gregorian days from the twenty-seventh of October to the eighth of November. There were thirteen.

He counted again and got the same result.

"A day is missing from the table," he said.

He took out Decree 1/I, which had come with the binders as an annex, and read it section by section. He stopped at section 5 and read it aloud.

"*Every fourth year there is a sixth additional day. Its name shall be laid down separately.*"

He looked at me.

"Has it been laid down?"

"No," I said.

"Year VIII is divisible by four."

"Yes."

"So Year VIII had a sixth additional day with no name."

"Yes."

He took the page of the Dock Book on which the Association's entry for 7.11. stood, and beside which I had written a question mark. He looked at the question mark for a long time.

"You marked this as an error."

"The conversion table is based on named days," I said. "That day had no name."

"So the day did not exist."

"Not according to the table."

He wrote nothing in his notebook. On the timeline he drew a line between Additional Day 5 and the first day of the Month of Promise. He wrote nothing beside the line, because there was no name to write.

---

"What happened on that day?" he asked.

No one answered, because the only ones who knew were sitting on Loading Dock 4.

He asked Paredes, who had come into the kitchen for the second time that day. Paredes looked at the timeline and at the empty place on it, and his voice was tired.

"El siete," he said. "El último día. Todavía se podía comer."

I understood the sentence. *Siete* is seven. *Último* is last. *Comer* is to eat. I knew what he had said.

The translation had been commissioned from the Ministry of the Future on the sixth day of the Month of Promise. A recorder has no authority to bypass a pending commission. I recorded:

*Witness answered in Spanish. Translation pending.*

Strömberg looked at me.

"Did you understand?"

"The translation is pending," I said.

That was true.

---

In the evening Strömberg sat alone at the timeline. The hearing of the Ministry of Finance was the next day. He had a motive, a contract, a beneficiary and twelve alibis.

He took out the IAEA record and read the footnote, which he had not read before, because it was a footnote. Then he looked at the snowman's sunglasses, which he had brought from the north gate that afternoon and placed on the table as evidence.

"Two instruments," he said. "At the same time, on the same glass."

He sat still for a while. Then he took binder 6 from the shelf, opened the frost man's notebook at the page for point 17 and, for the first time, read the figures instead of the remarks. He ran his finger down the column until it stopped at the twenty-seventh of October.

"Three," he said.

Then he said the sentence I recorded word for word, because it was the first time he doubted his own method:

"I have investigated this according to the state calendar. In the state calendar every day has a meeting. A day with no meeting does not exist. A day on which someone simply does something does not exist."

He tapped the empty line.

"This is not a timeline. It is a procedure."

He was silent for a moment.

"I have not been investigating this in spite of the wrong documents. I have been investigating it by means of them."

Then he took the Swedish statement from his breast pocket, opened the envelope and read it for the third time. This time he looked at the date.

I recorded: *Strömberg examined a document.*

---

Before going to bed V. took the scarf from his pocket and hung it over the back of a kitchen chair to dry.

Outside it was twenty degrees below zero. According to a thermal camera that no one was watching, the glass of the dome was glowing. In Honduras it was still raining.

---

## Onset of Thaw

*Recorder's note, the ninth day of the Month of Promise, Follow-on Funding Day (16.11.2034)*

Dupré called from Brussels at 7.40. No one had asked him to call.

Three days earlier Strömberg had asked him whether the archive of the Standing Committee held a statement by the Director-General of BANA concerning the bunch. Dupré had searched under *Bananna State* and replied that it did not. Now he called again.

"I was putting the archive in order," he said, in English with a French accent. "I do it every Thursday. I found a folder that no one has ever asked for. It is filed under *Banana State*. One *n*. It contains four pages, numbered 41 to 44."

"Where did it come from?" Strömberg asked.

"From the Committee's meeting of the sixth of November. As an annex. The Committee found the situation unchanged and did not open the annex. The annex was filed according to its spelling. The spelling was wrong, so it was filed in the wrong place." He paused. "Or in the right one. It depends who spells correctly."

He faxed the pages to the lodge. The fax machine was an old BANA device, and it worked.

The pages were K. Kapusiini's final statement on the bunch, dated 3.10.2034. On the last page there was one recommendation:

> *The bunch shall be eaten on the day it ripens, 2.1.2035. Clarification of ownership shall not delay eating. What is eaten is eaten.*

Strömberg read it aloud and put the paper down.

"The statement concerned a future day," he said. "So it was classified as an expectation. Expectations are referred to the Committee."

He stood up.

"Gather everyone at the dock. Twelve o'clock."

---

Everyone who had been invited came to Loading Dock 4. Eleven invitations had been sent, and eleven people came, which was unheard of in the history of the Bananna State.

Present were the Director General of the Ministry of Finance; the Development Director of the Ministry of the Future; the chair of the steering group for the bunch and of the steering group for the harvesting project (the same person); the secretary of the monitoring group; the insurer's local representative; Don Esteban Paredes; the chair and the eldest member of the Association; V.; and the undersigned. The last to arrive was the Director-General of BANA, K. Kapusiini. He was a capuchin, smaller than I had imagined. He sat down on the edge of the dock beside the Association.

Dupré was on the speakerphone.

At the far end of the dock stood a man in a frost-maintenance jacket with a thermal probe in his hand. He had not been invited. He had come because of a card left under a plant pot. No one asked him to leave.

Strömberg placed the snowman's sunglasses in the middle of the dock. No one asked why.

---

"Two weeks ago," Strömberg began, "I was told that the bunch had died fifty-five days before it ripened. I believed it, because it had been recorded. I looked for the person who had killed the bunch. I found a motive, a contract and a beneficiary."

He looked at the Director General of the Ministry of Finance.

"I was wrong."

The Director General said nothing. His expression was that of a man who is not sure whether he has been acquitted or accused.

"The bunch did not die before it ripened. It died after. No one killed it. It was waited to death."

---

He took BANA's original flowering notice from binder 2 and read it.

"*Flowering observed 17.6. VII.* In the archive index the date has been converted to 17.6.2033. But 17.6. VII does not mean the seventeenth of June. It is the seventeenth day of the Month of Establishment, Year VII, and by the Gregorian calendar that is the twenty-second of April 2033. The same day the Association entered in the Dock Book."

He put the paper down beside the sunglasses.

"There were not two flowerings, and not two observations. There was one flowering in two calendars. BANA's archive index read the state date as Gregorian. From then on every forecast was fifty-six days late."

"It is a technical error," said the Director-General of BANA quietly.

"It is," Strömberg said. "But it did not kill the bunch. Look at what would have happened without it."

He counted in his notebook.

"BANA's model gives 564 days from flowering to ripeness. Counted from the wrong date, that is the second of January 2035. Counted from the right one, it is the seventh of November 2034."

He looked at the Director-General.

"Even if the date had been read correctly, your statement would have recommended eating on the seventh of November. That day has no name. It is not in the state calendar. No meeting could have been convened for it to decide on eating."

K. Kapusiini looked at the boards of the dock.

"I know," he said. "Now."

---

"The bunch ripened on the twenty-seventh of October," Strömberg went on. "There were witnesses to that. No one listened to them."

He took a sheet from his folder. It was the hearing record of the eleventh. One word on it was underlined in pencil.

"I did not underline this," he said. "Someone read this before me."

The word was *weeping*.

"The snowman began to melt that day. When Greenhouse 3 reached ripening temperature, the glass warmed, and the snowman was standing against the glass. The puddle reached the glass and not away from it. The Association's entry for the twenty-seventh says: *The snowman is weeping.* That is not a figure of speech. It is a date."

V. looked at the dome. He did not say anything.

Strömberg took out the IAEA record.

"The satellite in Vienna measured the same glass on the same day. The record was filed because it did not concern nuclear power."

He turned toward the end of the dock.

"And the frost-maintenance officer measured it at point 17. Plus six point four degrees on the outside of the glass, at eight below. He reported it. He was told that the glass was heated as planned. His notebook was received and filed."

The man in the frost-maintenance jacket did not move.

Strömberg turned to Paredes.

"And you said it. Twice. In Spanish."

At that moment a Bananna State Post van stopped slowly at the end of the dock. The driver got out and handed an envelope to the nearest person, who was me.

It was from the Ministry of the Future. It contained the translation commissioned on the sixth day of the Month of Promise. I gave it to Strömberg, and he read it aloud:

> *The flower opened on the twenty-second of April. The bunch was ready on the twenty-seventh. Twelve days to eat it. Nobody came.*
>
> *The seventh. The last day. It could still have been eaten.*

Paredes nodded.

"Yes," he said. "That is what I said."

---

"Twelve days," Strömberg said. "The twenty-seventh of October to the seventh of November. And twelve decisions."

He read his table aloud, line by line, and with each line he looked at the person it concerned.

"Consultation Day. The pledgee sent its statement by post."

He took the Swedish statement from his breast pocket.

"Haparanda customs. My own office. The statement is dated the twenty-seventh of October, the day the bunch ripened. It says: *The pledgee does not object to harvest or consumption.* It was sent with a 25 B commemorative stamp and addressed to the bunch. It arrived sixteen days later, when the addressee was dead. I carried it in my pocket for a week and called it routine."

He laid the statement beside the sunglasses.

"Steering Group Day. Harvest once ownership is clear." He looked at the chair. "Interim Report Day. Ripening proceeding according to plan." The secretary. "Impact Day. A new classification." The Director-General. "Follow-on Funding Day. A pledge." The Director General of the Ministry.

He stopped at the sixth line.

"Establishment Day. *Harvest to await the independent assessment of the harvesting protocol. Commissioned from the Ministry of the Future.*"

He looked at me.

"Whose assessment?"

Everyone looked at me. Under the dock ran the exhaust duct, and its warmth reached the ankles.

"Ours," I said.

"You arrived on the ninth of November."

"Yes."

"You recorded this line without comment."

"It was not the recorder's task."

Strömberg said nothing. He did not need to.

I will write here what I did not say then. Throughout the investigation I had thought things through to the end. But I had thought them through by the rules of the machine, because I was standing inside it. I had not stood there before.

---

Strömberg read the remaining lines: the strategy day, the application, the founding of the steering group, the waiting for the funding decision, the Committee's finding. Each body had done something, and each had done it on time, according to the minutes.

"And the thirteenth day," he said. "The one with no name."

He turned to the Association.

"You were the only ones who did anything on a day that does not exist. You counted. You finished counting in the evening, and the answer was right. The bunch belonged to the eater. By morning there was nothing left to eat."

The chair did not look away.

"We were waiting for paper," he said. "Like everyone."

"In Christie's train," Strömberg said, to me and to no one else, "twelve passengers each struck once. Here each of them waited one day."

---

The Director General of the Ministry of Finance cleared his throat.

"The Ministry's position is that since everyone took part, no one is solely responsible."

"The Ministry's position is correct," Strömberg said. "That is the crime."

---

Last of all, Strömberg took out the inspection report, the one I had been writing, which he had asked me for that morning. He opened it at the first page.

"*The table of contents has been approved as a formality*," he read. "The table of contents is part of BANA's report template. Who prepared the template?"

"I did," said K. Kapusiini. "On the same day as the statement."

Strömberg read the chapter titles aloud. He read them slowly, and at each one he raised a finger.

"Entry. Association Hearing. The Night Dock. In Three Calendars. The Silent Witness. Nameless Day. Onset of Thaw. Winter, Afterwards."

He counted his fingers.

"E. A. T. I. T. N. O. W."

No one said anything.

"You wrote it twice," Strömberg said.

"Once in the text," said K. Kapusiini. "Once in the headings. In the Bananna State the headings are the only part everyone sees." He looked at the boards. "Neither was read."

For two weeks I had been writing chapters under those headings. I wrote them according to the document instructions. I did not read the headings. They were a formality.

---

Strömberg closed the folder. He looked at the Association's eldest member, who had sat on the dock the whole time without speaking.

"One last question," he said. "When did you understand that the bunch was dying?"

The eldest looked at Greenhouse 3 and then at Strömberg.

"When it could still be eaten."

---

There was one document Strömberg did not read aloud. It lay on top of his folder: the printout from binder 9, the access log of Airlock 3, open at its last page. One line had a time and no date.

| Date | Time | Event |
|---|---|---|
| | 18:15:00 | DATE INVALID. ACCESS PENDING. |

He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at the man at the end of the dock.

"Pending what?" he said.

No one answered. The log could not record a day that had no name, so it could not record what had been pending, or for whom, or whether anyone had come.

---

I recorded the answer of the eldest. Then I recorded the date, by the state calendar, because procedure required it: the ninth day of the Month of Promise, Follow-on Funding Day.

The sunglasses stayed on the dock. No one took them.

---

## Winter, Afterwards

*Recorder's note, the tenth day of the Month of Promise and after*

The insurer accepted Hargreaves's *force majeure* opinion three days after the dock. The opinion had arrived from London by email and reached the insurer before Strömberg's report. No compensation was paid.

The Ministry of Finance took note of the decision.

BANA added a new line to its balance sheet:

| Item | Value (BU) | Availability |
|---|--:|---|
| Bunch 1/2033, lost | 0 | Not available, as rotten |

It was the first line in BANA's history that corresponded to reality. K. Kapusiini signed it himself, under the heading *Banana State*, with one *n*.

The final statement stayed in Brussels. The Standing Committee met in December and found the situation unchanged. Dupré did not move the folder. He opened a second one beside it, filed under *Bananna State*, and put in it a reference to the first. There are now two folders in the archive, and both can be found.

Since pages 41–44 could not be attached to the inspection report, BANA approved a new official final statement for it. It was one line long:

> *The bunch ripened. The bunch rotted. The counting continued.*

---

The frost-maintenance officer's notebook was returned to him from binder 6 with a covering letter. The Frost Maintenance Office thanked him for his observations and informed him that point 17 had been added to the list of points measured as standard. The Remarks column had been removed from the form, as unnecessary.

---

Strömberg left on the morning train. He left the Swedish statement on the dock beside the sunglasses. He said that the statement had been addressed to the bunch and therefore belonged at the scene.

His report reached the Haparanda customs office a week later. It ran to thirty-four pages. Because it came from the Bananna State, it was cleared at the border under heading 4901, printed books and brochures. The clearance was carried out by Strömberg's own office. He signed for it himself.

---

We left last. The Ministry of the Future had still not cancelled our commission, but there was no longer anything to assess. The harvesting protocol had not been needed.

We drove home to the lake. It was already dark. The dock was under snow, and we left it as it was.

V. took the scarf from his pocket and hung it on the nail in the sauna porch, where there was room for it.

We sat in the kitchen for a while. No one recorded anything.

"Shall we heat the sauna?" I asked.

V. looked out of the window at the lake, under which the water was moving, though no one could see it.

"Let's."

---

*Inspection Report 1/IX ends.*
