Account 1/IX
Subject: Greenhouse 3, Row 14 – the disposition of Bunch 1/2033
Recorded by: A. Hämäläinen, junior frost-maintenance officer, capacity as stated on page one: *other*

*Archivist's note:* This account is filed next to Report 1/IX, with one blank line between them. Both carry the status Original. The archivist asked which of them concerns what happened. The Director-General replied that an account is the document in which a person says what he did, and that the archiving instructions, which the Office wrote, contain a rule for loss and a rule for disposal, and no rule, so far, for dinner.

## Eleven Seconds

I will begin with the last thing anyone said to me at a meeting, because everything after it was done in silence. On the twenty-ninth of October the monitoring group met at Loading Dock 4 and recorded that the ripening of bunch 1/2033 was proceeding according to plan, and when the minutes had been initialled and folded, Don Esteban Paredes of the Food and Agriculture Organization put on his coat and said, in slow, careful English, the English of a man who has decided never to be misunderstood about anything again:

"Where I come from, we eat."

He had been at Mustakorpi since 2031. He had grown bananas on the coast of Ecuador for forty years, and in all that time the state had never asked him anything, because a question is a form of spending and the year's spending had already been committed elsewhere. Nobody recorded the sentence. I am recording it here, at the one table in the file where a record can change what it counts, and I am dating it, because the date is the subject of this whole document, whether any of us like it or not.

The ministry's own last word on the bunch had come down on the first of November, at the end of the Month of Reporting, and it was doing as much work as a ministry sentence can do. The harvest of bunch 1/2033 was to await the independent assessment of the harvest protocol, commissioned from the Ministry of the Future. The assessment had not begun. The regular review period is fourteen months. The bunch had been ripe for five days, and its ripeness was accruing to the file as expectation, and nobody at the meeting that decided this had held a fruit in his hand since the last buffet the Ministry of Finance gave for its auditors. I was not at that meeting. I have been at meetings like it. I am invited to them as the officer responsible for the microclimates of row 14, which is to say I am invited as a thermometer. Thermometers have no opinions and no attendance rights, and at the last one I sat for four hours and fifty minutes being correct, which is the maximum a thermometer can give.

On the seventh of November I woke in the dark, an hour before the office opened, because my father was awake too, standing at his window on his bad knees, and he had said, of nothing, "The snowman is gone." When my father says a thing like that at five in the morning, he does not mean it as weather. I drove north with the probe. There was indeed no snowman at the north gate. There was a dish of ice on the mud, and the sunglasses, and the scarf, and the carrot standing upright in the dish, like an instrument between measurements. I walked the thirty-two sample points of the north route and wrote down the temperatures, because the alternative was to write down the other thing, and the notebook is not the place for the other thing. The notebook is a measuring instrument, and I am the part of it that can be surprised.

The Association was at the dock by eight, on the warm deck where the data centre's exhaust duct runs, at the page where the evening column was still empty. It was the eleventh morning of the same number. The morning count went in at 08:40: 142, yellow, soft, and the remark column, which the chair keeps the way other chairs keep voting records, says that the smell of the bunch had begun to leave the glass. Ripeness is a state of verbs. By the evening it would be a state of numbers, or close to it, and by the next morning it would be a state of the past, and none of that was knowable at eight in the morning, which is the only hour the country is any good at.

I stayed. I will not pretend I had a procedure for staying. The dock is the warmest outdoor place at Mustakorpi, and my day had been scheduled against the north route, and the north route ends at the dock, and I had, at the end of it, a probe in my hand and no meeting left in the day to be correct at. The capuchins had not left either. They had not left since the flowering, in the spring of 2033, when the first flower opened on row 14 and was recorded — in the Dock Book, on the day it was, and in BANA's index under the wrong year — and the sense of the thing that those two records together were telling had not yet been given permission to mean anything, because permission in this country is a document, and there was no document for meaning. I was not in this story yet. I came north the following February, and everything I know about the flowering I know from the two records, which agree about the flower and disagree about the year.

The probe had spent the day doing the work it was issued for. Point 17, the north gate, read +7.2 early and had fallen by the time I passed it again going home, and the difference between those two numbers is the nearest thing the north route has to an evening. I recorded both, and under them, in the remarks, I wrote: *snowman gone; effects on ice lens.* The Office's rules are strict about remarks. They are for weather and for conditions, and in the nine months since I came north I have never once been stopped for a remark about a snowman.

At 18:14 the Dock Book lay open at a page with an empty evening column. Ten metres away, beside the airlock of Greenhouse 3, a small display showed two lines: the time and the date. Behind the glass, at the height of a man's chest, hung the bunch. At 18:15 the date line went blank. No error message appeared. The system does not show what it has not been given, and it had not been given a name for the day, so it showed nothing, and the time kept running, and then the door opened.

The door opened. The system never once asked me who I was. It did not know which day it was converting, so it did not know which procedures were in force, and when it has no procedure it has no rule, and when it has no rule the door does what the code does when a condition arrives that nobody on earth wrote a line for: what it has never done before, and cannot do twice, because the day that triggered it will never have a name. The display said one thing that evening, in letters the size of a stamp:

*DATE INVALID. ACCESS GRANTED (DEFAULT)*.

Behind me, on the warm deck, the Association watched me cross the ten metres that no document will ever be able to log. They did not move. They had not been asked, either.

Inside the warm strip the glass was at +31.2 degrees, and I will say once, here, what I say on training courses about heat in general: heat is a moralist. It goes to the thing that is ripe. It does not consult the ledger, because you cannot serve a document on a temperature, and it had spent eleven days making the only honest point in the whole file, which is that the fruit was over.

The bunch hung at the height of my own chest. Eleven hands of fruit, heavy, the colour of the inside of a warm room, and the smell that I am not going to describe, because the words we have for smell are the words we have for rooms that somebody has just left. On the metal table at the south end lay the insurance company's folder of compensation offers, unsigned since September, patient as a form. The floor was warm where the discharge pipe enters, in a circle the ice never reaches, and on that circle, at a quarter past six in the evening of a day with no name, there was nobody and nothing except a bunch of bananas, a folder, a probe and me.

No one had a right to be in that room. There was no pass, no annex, no harvest right, no clause in any drawer of this state entitling a person to eat a banana; I have said that already, and I will go on saying it, because it is the one part of this account that an entire country has tried, since November, to make into the important part, and it is not. What there was, was a door that had opened. Under the constitutional ruling of the third of March 2027, a banana that exists may be eaten by whoever gets there first, provided it is not a future banana. I have read the ruling. It sits in the folder Aila Palojärvi keeps for papers drawn up in good faith. I am able to state as a technician that the banana was not a future banana. It was present. It had a year and a half of paperwork describing itself as a growing expectation, and on the evening of the seventh of November the expectation, and the object, and the price were, for one hour, the same thing.

I took one banana out of the second hand from the stem, because the second hand from the stem comes free. I peeled it standing up, still holding the probe in the other hand, the way you hold a tool when you have been sent to a meeting to hold a tool. Nobody stopped me. Nobody came to stop me, and nobody has since suggested that they were on their way to. The peel went into the pocket of my jacket, because the room had no bin, and because I already knew, without knowing how I knew, that the peel would be the second hardest object of the year for the state's books to hold.

It tasted like a banana.

I have read Section 2 of the constitution of Bannanivaltio. It forbids the eating of future bananas. I have no talent for the law and have never needed one; the section is a sentence about time, and the banana was a thing about time that had run out, politely, in front of everyone.

At 18:31, in my blue notebook, standing at the end of the dock, I wrote the entry that this file exists around. It is complete and exact and it took eleven seconds to write, which I know because the Office's clocks run to the second and I was calm enough, by then, to look:

| Time | Entry |
|---|---|
| 18:31:12 | 07.11. No name. Greenhouse 3, row 14: +31.2 °C. Bunch: 141. One banana eaten. Recorded as consumption. By me. |

Colleagues at the Ministry of Finance have pointed out, in good faith, that the entry is not long enough to be a document. It has no reference, no legal basis, and no copies. That is true. It has the other thing, which is a date, and a count, and a name; and of the three, the one the state's books had never carried before the seventh of November 2034 is the name.

My father did not eat. My father was at Neulamäki, seventy-two years old, at his window, and had not been asked, and would not have come. Nobody knows that better than I do. It belongs to the last chapter of this account, and I will not spend it here.

## Audit

The Association counted, in the evening, as it had counted every evening since the spring meeting of 2033 decided that the country's arithmetic ought to have a floor. The evening column of the Dock Book for the seventh of November — empty when I saw it in the morning, because the morning belongs to the promise — is not empty now, and I set the row down as it stands:

| Date | Trucks | Bananas | Remarks |
|---|--:|--:|---|
| 7.11. | 0 | 141 | Evening count. One eaten. One peel. Not export. |

The peel went to the chair of the Association at the dock, because I did not know which bin exists for it, and I was right about that: no bin exists for it. The chair held it up to the light the way a customs officer holds a banknote, entered it in the Dock Book, and put it under the column the society's book has kept since 2030, when the only fruit the port of the state ever shipped was a peel from the Prime Minister's residence. Not export. Given.

At 06:14 on the morning of the eighth of November the bunch was found, by the same member of the Association who had watched it flower, brown and soft where it hung, all one hundred and forty-one skins still whole. He did not touch it. He walked back to the dock and wrote it down, and the Association's entry for that morning is in the file: *Bunch. 0 edible.* Of the finest tropical power of the European Union, the part that remained was 141 bananas, inedible by breakfast; the only member of the state who had eaten a banana of Bannanivaltio was the man writing this account; and the state's files now say so, which means that for one line, the files were ahead of the radio.

Ilmari Virtanen telephoned the office that morning. He retired in the summer, after thirty-four years of standing in weather, and the office answered him on the second ring, which nobody under this roof can remember happening before. He asked one question.

"Was it yellow?"

"It was."

"Then it was ready," he said, "and the rest is administration." Then he rang off. That is the complete text of Ilmari's view of November 2034, and I state, for the balance of the file, that it is also mine, given at a length reduced to fit the format.

A. Strömberg, customs inspector, Kingdom of Sweden, Haparanda, arrived on the morning of the ninth, a Preparation Day, by the same train as the assessor K., commissioned by the Ministry of the Future to evaluate a harvesting protocol for a fruit that had ceased to be an assessment subject two days earlier, and by V., whose name is on the commission and whose voice is not anywhere in it. They had been sent before the seventh of November and could not be un-sent. That is a state of verbs: the commission had been commissioned, the assessment had been issued, and none of the verbs has an undo.

Strömberg asked for the papers of the bunch. He received 1,200 pages in fourteen binders, and he read them, in two days, at the kitchen table of the Puijo lodge, with a thermos at his elbow. He was the first person ever to do this. I carried the binders, and I saw the table: the life of the bunch in fourteen binders, the flowering notice with its two dates, the conversion tables, the minutes of eleven meetings, the insurance schedule with the compensation terms, the state calendar in fourteen pages, one of which has a hole in it. Nobody had ever assembled them in one room, and no one had ever read them all. Both facts were true for the whole history of the file up to the second week of November 2034.

On the second morning he sent for the state calendar, all fourteen pages, and spread them across the kitchen table so that they overlapped at the corners, and turned to the last one and looked for a long time at the space at the end of it where the sixth additional day has its number and no name.

"In Sweden," he said, "we put the extra day in February and gave it a name, and now it is nobody's favourite and nobody's problem. It is not the law that makes a calendar work. It is whether somebody agreed to it. This calendar has never been agreed to by anyone who has been cold."

He did not write it in the notebook. I did.

On the second evening he called me in. He had the Dock Book open, and the flowering notice open beside it, and I stood in the doorway, because the chair on the far side of the table was next to a radiator and I had had one meeting furnished at my expense already that month.

He wanted the sequence read into the record. I read it. The morning count of the seventh of November: 142. The evening count of the seventh: 141, one eaten, peel one, not export. The morning count of the eighth: 141, of which 0 edible. He wrote as I read, and then he closed the Dock Book, and then he did the arithmetic out loud, in the slow way, the way that has fingers in it.

"Two days ago," he said, "I would have found in these books the papers of a bunch that was promised, pledged, forecast, insured, classified through the ripe growing expectation, and filed. The books would have totalled 142, and the number on the page would have been the number in the books, because the page and the books are written by the same hand. Today the books say 141 and one eaten, and the pile is rotten, and the numbers match. I have been reading this state's papers for two days. This is the first evening on which they have balanced."

He put the access log of the airlock on the table, binder 9, last page. One line. A time, and no date: 18:15:00, *DATE INVALID. ACCESS GRANTED (DEFAULT)*.

"The log cannot record what happened," he said. "It has no day to put on it. So I will put it in words instead. Neither document in this file can be disproved, and I am not in the business of belief. I am the pledgee's inspector, and I am informing the parties that the pledgee does not intend to choose. The pledgee counts. The count of the pledgee's collateral, over the period examined, is: 142, eleven mornings running; then, on the evening of the seventh, 141 and one eaten; then 141, standing, rotten. The count closes. Nothing in file 1/IX alters the count. The rest is weather."

He looked at the constitutional ruling for a long moment, the way men look at a shelf they are not going to climb.

"Where I come from," he said, "the inspector fit for this matter would be the counter."

On the eleventh, Stakeholder Day, he held the hearing the books did not require, on the dock, because the witnesses in this matter will not leave sight of the scene. He asked the chair whether the evening count had been checked. The chair said, "We counted." He asked who would certify the count. The chair looked at me. Nobody wrote anything down. T. Kapusiini has been the chair of a registered association for six years and knows exactly how long a sentence needs to be to survive a filing system, and his were the shortest. The inspector thanked him. He said that the thank-you was not transferable.

K. and V. came by the office the next day, and closed the commission in an afternoon, which is, by the measure of the ministry that issued it, the fastest closing in the history of its commissions. K. had come an hour up the lake road to evaluate a harvesting protocol for a bunch that was by then compost. He did, in an afternoon, what the state's machinery around the bunch had not managed since the bunch was first an expectation: he wrote the closing line. It is one sentence, and it is in the file, and I give it here because it is the only sentence the Ministry of the Future issued in 2034 that does not contain the word *ongoing*:

> Object of assessment: none. Cause: the bunch is not. Recommendation: none. Commission closed.

V. read my blue notebook while K. wrote. He read the eleven seconds, and the row of the Dock Book, and the temperature column, and he was a long time over the temperature column, and then he gave the notebook back and nodded. I did not write down what the nod was about, because it was not an entry. It was the only transaction of that whole fortnight in which the goods and the receipt passed in the same second, and neither of them was paper.

The books of the bunch, in the version of the file that carries this account, therefore close as follows, and I give the closing lines because they are the shortest thing BANA has ever let me put on paper:

| Item | Count | Status |
|---|--:|---|
| Bunch 1/2033, 7.11. morning | 142 | ripe |
| Bunch 1/2033, 7.11. evening | 141 | plus one, in a private capacity, hunger |
| Bunch 1/2033, 8.11. | 141 | 0 edible |

Balanced. Not saved. The balance sheet should be read with both of those words in it, because a reader who has known a bunch when it is ripe will read the first, and a reader who has ever read the files of this state will already have guessed the second.

## The Field With No Box

On the first working day of December a form arrived at the Frost Maintenance Office, four pages, blue, addressed to me personally, with a reference, a colour scheme and under four weeks of working group behind it, and I will be fair to it, because whatever else this file does it will not say that the state did not try. The heading of the form is long and the section numbers are real. I give the operative title only:

> NOTIFICATION OF CONSUMPTION OF A BANANA OF THE STATE, PURSUANT TO SECTION 2 OF THE CONSTITUTION AND DECISION BANA-KKO/2034/1

Page one asks who consumed. Page two asks which banana, by row, by hand, and by position within the hand; the state's taxonomy does not descend below the finger, and it does, in fact, have a field for the finger, which is one more field than it has for the eater. Page three asks when the consumption took place, and offers, in the electronic version, a dropdown listing the ten named days of the state week in each of the twelve named months of the year. Page four asks the value of what was consumed, in Banana Units, entered by the consumer.

Pages one and two I filled in at my desk, in the Month of Promise of Year IX. Under "capacity in which consumed," the field would not accept "as a private person," and accepted "other," and for "other" it opened a free-text field, and the free text I entered was one word, and the word was accepted, because a text field will take anything, and that is the whole difference between a person and a form.

Page three stopped both halves of the state that meet in a dropdown. The seventh of November 2034 is not the tenth day of any week and not the thirtieth day of any month. It is the sixth additional day of Year VIII, the one the decree says shall be named separately, which has never been done. I wrote "the day that has no name." The electronic version rejected it, on the ground that it was not on the list. The paper version accepted it, on the ground that the clerk who reads the paper version has a family and knows what November is.

Page four I did not touch for three days, and I will give the three days the length they deserve, which by the standards of this file is one paragraph. A value field is the point in a document where the state asks a person to translate something into the currency, and in a country with 2.4 billion units of debt against fruit that does not exist, the translation of one eaten banana into Banana Units is not arithmetic. It is the question of what anything in this country was worth that was not pledged, and the honest answer is the one the form was not built to hold. I had, in my jacket pocket, at home, the peel of the only banana, and the state had, in a classification of thirty-four pages adopted on the thirtieth of October, a ripeness model, and ripeness models are what a state legislates when it intends to eat later. Four pages of form was, by the measure of the KKO, a triumph of economy.

On the third day Aila Palojärvi came to my desk, looked at the empty page four, and took the pen out of my hand.

"You are not the value," she said. "You're the event. The value was the state's. That is what the state is for: pouring value into things that cannot pay it back and calling the pour a forecast."

She wrote page four herself, in her own hand, which is the hand the audit office accepts on sight, and the figure under it was:

*One banana. Market value at the world price prevailing on the day of consumption, plus the hurricane adjustment then pending: 0.0000004 BY. Not priced. Livelihood of the consumer: unchanged. Net effect on the national accounts: the state had one fewer banana than it believed, and one more true number than it has ever had before.*

The form went to the audit office, and the audit office, which cannot audit a person without first auditing the form, audited the form. On the eighteenth of December it issued the decision that closed notification ACK/2034/1, and I give it in full, from the file copy, because the newspapers quoted only the third sentence and the third sentence is the least of it:

> "Notification ACK/2034/1 reports the first verified consumption of a banana of the State of Bannanivaltio in the history of the state. The Office notes that the verified figure is smaller than the smallest figure previously carried in the accounts by a factor that the Office's software declines to display. The Office further notes that it is the only figure in the national accounts verified by two independent instruments, namely the person who ate it and the association that counted to 141. The Office has no category for this figure, because no category for this figure has ever been required, the reason being that the state has never before had an inventoriable object and a stomach at the same address. The Office creates the category. The category is: EATEN. The figure is entered under EATEN and nowhere else. No future banana is affected. Closed."

The decision is half a page long. The classification it sits beside, the ripe growing expectation, is thirty-four pages, and required three committees. There is a proportionality section in the Office's own instructions, and I have read it, and it does not apply to gratitude.

The peel was raised at the audit office, by me, in the annex to the notification, and the annex is one sentence: *one peel, given, not export.* The Office ruled that the peel travels with its banana, so that the reference must, in the printed register, cite the fruit and the peel in the same line, and the line beneath the category reads, in the smallest academic hand the Office possesses: *peel: follows fruit.*

On the same day, under the balance sheet of the state, a line appeared which I have scanned, and which Aila will confirm is the first closing line of a bunch in this country's history that survived contact with the object it describes:

| Item | Value | Closing position |
|---|---|---|
| Bunch 1/2033 | 141, lost; 1, eaten; 0 BY | Not available, as rotten. One verified. |

K. Kapusiini signed the decision himself, with one n. The file does not say whose n, and the file never will.

## Eighth Inquiry

In the third week of November the office received, by genuine post, the eighth enquiry of Bernt Engen, harbour master of Narvik.

The first seven are in the archive of this file, and I will not repeat them; they are the property of a man, not a ministry, and I have read them all. The first asked, in 2027, for a sailing list for the first spring of the Bannanivaltio Silk Road. The fourth asked for intentions. The sixth came with a photograph of the berth, empty, in snow, with the ice on the bollards, and a caption in a tidy hand: *as at this date.* Their grammar is the grammar of a port, tonnage sought, manifest sought, dispositions sought, and their courtesy has increased each year with the answer "a study is enclosed." The seventh closed with a sentence that will never be found by the Ministry of the Future, because it is filed in another country: *I am aware that your cargo is not prevented by anything at my end.*

The eighth was shorter. A harbour master who has been answered in studies learns what the answers do for a living, and the eighth enquiry asks, in his own hand, one question only:

> Is there a shipment?

The state's answer to that question has existed since 2027, and it is not a lie, and it has never been a lie. The first shipment of the Silk Road is expected upon completion of the current phase of the export readiness programme. The programme exists, and it has phases, and one of them completes every year, and a port cannot moor a phase. Over the life of the correspondence the answer has been revised six times, and each revision is longer than the one before, and the total effect of eight years of it is that the berth is held, the studies are read, and the reply to a question about ships remains a small claim on the future, which is what this country mines instead of ore.

In December, after file 1/IX had been divided and stamped, the file came round to the office with the eighth enquiry attached and a routing slip typed by a machine. With it came the ministry's own draft answer, four pages, with a reference to the readiness programme in its third phase and a table of the phases running to the fifth, and a covering instruction that the reply was to be sent "in the state's usual form." I have nothing against the state's usual form. I have held it. It weighs more than the peel.

The machine's routing slip, with the honesty of all machines asked a question in their sleep, had addressed it to: *frost officer (other).*

I gave the answer the file had by then prepared me for, because the only thing I had that the state's answer did not have was a category with a verified figure in it. I wrote it at my desk, on the office's own headed paper, and Aila read it through once before she stamped it, which she does not do, and said, "Send it before somebody annexes you to a working group," and posted it in the slot marked *official*, which by then I was, on a form somewhere, and it went out on time.

> Dear Sir,
>
> In answer to your eighth enquiry, which I answer in the capacity stated on file: there is no shipment.
>
> There will not be one in my working life. The country's total harvest for the current year was one banana, of which the books record, verified: one eaten, one hundred and forty-one lost as rotten. The port is asked to hold no berth for fruit that a numerical system of this state's size, at this state's temperatures, has never been able to deliver past its own accounts.
>
> I am sorry about the berth.
>
> A. Hämäläinen, Frost Maintenance Office (other)

There has been no reply. I did not expect one. A port that has read eight years of studies can tell the difference between a study and an answer, and between an answer and the truth, and the truth is that the books balance from the Narvik end too: the harbour master asked what would be coming across the water, and what was coming across the water was, at the date of this account, exactly what the state's own verified column said it was.

Exports are a state of verbs. You ship, you pledge, you certify, you announce; the crates move, and the papers move, and somewhere there is always a quay, and on the quay a person holding a document that describes what is not there. That is the country's way of touching the sea. It has been, since 2027, one of the two ways in this file, and the other, which weighed 141 grams without its peel, went into a stomach at 61 degrees north, and only one of the two can be repeated next year, and the port knows which.

Ilmari read the answer before it went, over my shoulder, at the radiator, where he has been having opinions since before I was hired. He had no comment on the sentence. He did say, as I put on my coat, the one thing I would not have dared to put in the letter, and I give it as Ilmari's, since a pension is the only rank in this country entitled to say it:

"They will keep the berth anyway. Berths are cheap. Hope is not a line item."

## Neulamäki

My father's window is on the north side of the house at Neulamäki, a big thermopane, and on the frame of it there is a pencil mark in my father's hand with the word *ammunition* written beside it. The story of the mark is this. In the year the window was put in, a man in a shop, asked what a house in this country needs for the winter, said the word, and my father — who was forty at the time and did not undertake operations without a record — went home and marked the frame and wrote the word next to it. I have been shorter than the mark for almost the whole of my life. On the evening of the eighth of November 2034 I went home, and I stood at the window, next to the mark, and my father was at the window too, because at dusk in November the dome of Mustakorpi lights up from the inside like a low warm moon eleven kilometres north, and he is not interested in it, and has never once missed it.

I told him I had eaten one.

He did not ask which. He did not ask how it tasted; and I note for the file that one hour earlier, in an office eleven kilometres north, a form with a value field and a subsection had asked me precisely that, twice. He looked out at the lit glass on the horizon for a while, his weight on his knees in the way that knees take weight when the man behind them has decided not to move, and he said the thing I am going to end this account with, because it is the sentence he had room to say, being the only person in the whole file who is not holding an instrument:

"I know the look of a plant that is being counted instead of picked."

That is the complete text of his evidence on the seventh and eighth of November 2034. He then asked whether I had peeled it properly, and I said that I had. He said that a man who peels properly can be trusted with the rest of it.

And then he did the thing I have kept for the end of this part. He put one finger on the pencil mark at the edge of the window — the word the man in the shop had used, forty years ago, for what a house in this country needs for the winter — and read it out, once, at the speed of a man checking a figure against a column:

"Ammunition."

In the lifetime of one bunch the state had classified it as an expectation, a pledge, a collateral, a forecast, a ripe growing expectation, and a loss. Never as ammunition. Ammunition is the thing a house keeps instead of using — kept in working order, one round to a house — until the day arrives that the calendar cannot name, and on that day it is spent. The state's arsenal sat in a barrel for 564 days, at +31.2 degrees on the last of them, and on the evening of the seventh of November it fired the one round it actually had, from the second hand from the stem, through a door that opened because no rule was in force. My father had written the word as an answer to a question nobody had asked yet. The question came home at dusk, in a frost-maintenance jacket, having measured its way down the north route twice in one day, and it conducted itself properly, and it peeled correctly.

The snowman he had seen go at dawn he saw go first from this window, thirteen hours before I did, and I record the order of the two observations because a file should be exact about who has instruments and who has a window. At five in the morning he had said, of nothing, "The snowman is gone," in the kitchen, and had watched me drive north with a probe. At dusk the sunglasses, the scarf and the carrot still lay on the ice lens at the north gate, uncollected, because no form exists for the effects of a snowman, and the object therefore continued, in law, to be weather.

I sat in the kitchen while he made coffee he did not offer me, and did the arithmetic he did not ask for. The bunch's papers had promised 142 bananas. The bunch delivered 142 bananas: 141 to nobody, and 1 to a man whose job it is to notice things at −8 degrees in the dark. Of the promises made in the last eight years by the tropical superpower of the European Union, that is the ratio: the promise is kept, and then some, and then it is filed.

He went up at nine. I sat at the kitchen table until the lit dome went dark on the horizon, because the supplementary lamps of Mustakorpi go off at ten, and after ten the row you cannot help watching is the one thing at Neulamäki that is warmer than the kitchen. An account should be written the day it is given. I wrote it, and I started it with the count, which is the shortest sentence I know and the only one that has never needed a phase.

On the Monday the frost front came down off Puijo in the usual way, and I walked the thirty-two points at dawn, and the notebook read like any notebook: −2.1 at the north gate and −4 outside it, the ice lens holding, the carrot upright. Aila stamped the page RECEIVED. FILED., and the file took both, the way it has begun to.
