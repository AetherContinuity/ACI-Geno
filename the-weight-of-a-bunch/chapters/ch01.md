# Part One: The Data of Ice

## Chapter 1: The Man Who Measured Frost

I came back to Kuopio in February of 2034 with one bag and no plans, which is the standard configuration for anyone who has ever left Kuopio. The bus from the airport stopped at the travel centre, and I stood in the -19° cold holding the bag and trying to remember whether the lake was supposed to be on my left or my right. It was on both sides. The lake had frozen in every direction and offered itself as a road, and a man was crossing it on a snowmobile with two children on the back seat, going somewhere that was neither direction. That was the first thing I understood about home: directions do not matter here. Winter matters. Everything else is arrangement.

My name is Akseli Hämäläinen. I am thirty-six years old, and until that February I had spent nine years in a windowless room in Oulu maintaining the cooling systems of a data centre that was owned by people who had never seen it. My job had been to keep the pipes from freezing, which in Oulu describes about forty percent of all human work. I know how heat moves, how it runs to the edges where you lose it, how it pools where you least want it and refuses to gather where you explicitly asked. Heat is a moralist. It goes exactly where it wants.

My father had called in November. He said his knees were finished and that I should come home, and he said it in the particular voice of a man who has decided a thing and is using his knees as an official pretext. I did not argue. In nine years I had learned the one thing a data centre can teach: no machine, however expensive, is ever as warm as the room it is not in.

My father used to work in orchards. Not the greenhouse — those didn't exist then — but the small farms southeast of the city, the ones that grow the blackcurrants nobody picks anymore because the pickers are now employed to fill forms about the blackcurrants nobody picked. He has the hands for it. I have his hands, which is to say that when I shake hands with people they always look a little disturbed.

My first job in the city, standing at the employment desk with my cooling certificate and my nine years, was at a place I had walked past a thousand times and never thought about: the Frost Maintenance Office of the State of Bannanivaltio. I'll allow that sentence a moment. I walked past the office myself before reading the sign, read the sign, and put the words in the order that went: Bannanivaltio, Frost, Maintenance. Then I went back and stood in front of it.

The office was on the ground floor of the Mustakorpi administrative building, which itself was on the ground floor of the Mustakorpi waste-heat region, which itself is in Finland, specifically between the north shore of Kallavesi and the west slope of Puijo. If you know the lake and the hill, you know the region. If you don't, no further description will help you.

The man behind the desk was named Ilmari Virtanen, in his late sixties, with the lungs of a lifetime smoker and the shoes of a lifetime walker. He asked me two questions. Did I know how heat moves, and was I willing to write things down.

I said I knew how heat moves, and I said I wrote things down anyway.

He said, "Good. Everyone in this state writes things down, and no one knows how heat moves."

He explained what the office did. In 2026, before I was born out of the industry, an investment consortium had built a three-hundred-hectare tropical greenhouse complex at Mustakorpi. The heat for the greenhouse came from the waste heat of a data centre, the power came from windmills and a small nuclear reactor that had not yet been built, and the whole thing had been sold to the Ministry of Finance as a "self-fulfilling circular ecosystem." The name of the state was in the paperwork: Banaanivaltio. One n, in the association documents; two ns, in the state documents. This will be relevant later.

The greenhouse had been running for eight years. In that time it had produced, officially, two point three million bananas for export and, unofficially, fourteen bunches a year, of which eleven had gone bad and three had been eaten at the opening coffee of the Prime Minister's residence. That was the entire industrial history of what is now the largest tropical superpower in Northern Europe.

"What did you produce in Oulu?" Ilmari asked me.

"Data," I said.

"Where did the data go?"

"To the customers."

"And the heat?"

"To the greenhouse at Mustakorpi."

"So you were a farmer."

I said I was not a farmer. He said that anyone whose job ends in a greenhouse is a farmer, and he gave me a clipboard and a card with a code on it, and I became the junior frost-maintenance man of the State of Bannanivaltio.

Nobody gave me a key. The key to the greenhouse complex was held by the greenhouse itself, which, like all complex systems in this state, maintained its own paperwork. I was given a thermal probe and a notebook with a blue cover, and Ilmari pointed across the parking lot toward a thin ribbon of heat rising from the forest floor like a scar of warm air, and said that the heat went there and that I should follow it.

I followed it for four hours.

It was my first walk with waste heat as a trail, and I would not trade that walk for any of the nine years in Oulu. The snow was three tiers of blue-grey. Under a spruce there was a strip of bare moss that never froze because of the pipe below, and in the strip there was a green leaf, a leaf alone, out of context, the way a teacup sits alone in a lake. I had to look at that leaf for a while. Something had decided to grow. In this country. In February.

At the greenhouse, the heat ran into the wall, and the wall was forty metres of glass, and behind the glass it was, according to my probe, +28°C. Ahead of me, in the direction the heat was going, was the whole forest.

I opened the notebook. I wrote: "Frost-maintenance walk, week 7. Waste heat exits data centre at 61°N. Product temperature at greenhouse gate, +28°C. Snow line begins at glass wall. Outside, -17°C. Leaf found on ground. One." I closed the notebook and stood there.

Somewhere behind me, in the direction of Puijo, there was a man's laugh, and then a shape came running along the edge of the treeline. Two shapes. Dogs, I thought. Then the dogs turned, and the dogs had faces, and the faces had eyebrows, and I learned on that first day that the State of Bannanivaltio had an operational capuchin population. I stood there with my clipboard and my writing, and a capuchin monkey sat on a snowbank four metres from me and looked at me with the expression of a customs official who has concluded that you are not worth the paperwork.

Later I learned that this monkey was probably a descendent of the original escape of 2026, that there are by now around thirty of these free-living individuals on Puijo, that the State of Bannanivaltio does not own them because no one owns the State of Bannanivaltio's citizens, and that in 2029 they had been entered into the municipal voting register, one ape one vote, tails not counted.

I wrote: "Two capuchins sighted. Not affected by cold. One looked at me as though I were a bystander."

Then I walked home, and the snow was easy, and I felt for the first time in nine years that the work I was doing was work that a person could actually point at.

That was the last time I felt that, at least at Mustakorpi.

## Chapter 2: The Ledger and the Light

My father lives in Neulamäki in a wooden house with a big thermopane window on the south side. On the window frame, from my childhood, there is still a pencil mark with the word "ammunition" — someone in a shop described frost exactly that way once, and he thought it deserved recording. He is seventy-two, he is proud of his knees, and his knees are finished.

The house stands on the third level of a small slope, which means the front door opens on the ground and the back door opens on the air four metres above the slope, and in his youth the back door was a ladder and in his old age it is a rail and a promise of winter stairs. The greenhouse at Mustakorpi is not visible from the house, but the hill of Puijo is, and in the evenings the top of the hill now has a faint amber glow, and if you know what that glow is — the waste heat house, the light of the data centre's permanent evening — you notice that the whole city has grown used to it. Nobody looks at it. Only strangers do.

I looked at it the first night I was home, and I remember thinking: it was warmer here once. Or it will be. It's one or the other.

The second day at the Frost Maintenance Office I was given the work that would be mine for a year, though I didn't know it then. There are four weeks in the State of Bannanivaltio's month, and the months have names such as Promise, Project, Growth, Study, Pilot, Establishment, Continuity, Strategy, Innovation, Impact, Networking, and Reporting. Ilmari handed me a laminated sheet that explained this, and I understand why a state that lives on reports would want a calendar that makes every week sound like a grant application. It was not a joke. It had been decided in 2032, without competition, without peer review, and without unnecessary citizen consultation.

My job was the cold side of the state. The greenhouse has a huge heat budget. The budget is funded by the data centre's waste heat, and the data centre's budget is funded by the cash flow of the customers in Oulu and in Nebraska, and the customers in Nebraska are funded by general social systems that have nothing to do with a greenhouse in Savonia. But the budget has a hole at the edges. Heat leaves at the edges. The greenhouse is forty metres of glass, and in a Savo winter every square metre of that glass is the boundary between +28°C and -25°C, and every metre is a furnace for heating the outdoors.

The engineers had proposed a heat curtain. The Ministry of Finance had declined it as a marginal cost. The audit office had recommended a study. The study had recommended a second study. The second study had come back and said that the heat loss could be reduced by 11.3 percent if the glass were frosted, and that the frosting would, coincidentally, improve the privacy of the monkeys.

The frosting solution was achieved in 2031, and it worked, for one year. Then the capuchins learned that a frosted pane can be scraped with a fingernail, and they scraped it, and the glass went clear again, and the monkeys watched the snow fall at -20° and sat inside at +28°, and everyone agreed that the problem was an efficiency question, not a pride question, and the study to resolve the pride question was commissioned in 2033 and is still underway.

My work, as a frost man, was to walk the perimeter of the greenhouse every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, at dawn, when the temperature gradient is at its steepest, and measure the frost front at thirty-two sample points, and record it, and hand the notebook to a woman named Aila Palojärvi, who is not a frost woman but who is the only person at Mustakorpi who reads all the notebooks in one place. She is about my age, with the kind of face that has refused to develop opinions, and she reads the notebooks and enters their data into the state database, and then the state database does not care, but that is not her business. She is paid to read. I am paid to write.

At one of the points, on the north side, we were meant to walk in a section of forest between the west wall of the greenhouse and the east slope of Puijo. Through the middle of this section ran the discharge pipe of the third coolant line of the data centre, a pipe that never stops exhaling. It has been exhaling since 2026. At the edge of its breath there is a moss strip, forty centimetres wide, that stays at +3° all winter, and in the strip lives the ecosystem of one banana plant's fallen leaf, autumn after autumn.

The first Monday in March, in week X / State month "Reporting," Aila walked with me and I learned the second fact of the state. The first fact is that a monkey is not a taxpayer. The second fact is that forty centimetres of moss is a deficit.

We stopped at the moss strip. There was a flower spike in the strip, because there is always something, and Aila types the words verbatim into the database: "flower spike, possible, needs 120 days, April." And then, two weeks later, it will be "flower spike, certain, 140 days" and then "germination observed" and so on, and none of the entries will ever become an object. It is a state of verbs.

Growing in that strip, out of the small red mud of the discharge pipe, was a small banana plant. Not seed. A leaf. A leaf that had fallen from row 14 of greenhouse 3 and ended up, by wind or by monkey, in the pipe's warm microclimate, and had done the incomprehensible thing: it had rooted.

I looked at it for a long time. I am not a sentimental man, but I will report that I stood there and felt that the little leaf was the only fully honest thing in the state.

Aila took the notebook and wrote in it — not in my column, in hers — "1 banana plant, spontaneous, will not be recorded in the national accounts." Then she looked up at me, and I said what the data said.

"Will it survive?"

Aila looked at the strip. Snow on three sides, +3° at the root, -17° at the tips, and the pipe that never breathes out but always breathes warm.

"It will survive the way the state does," she said. "It will be reported."

## Chapter 3: The State of the Reports

I should explain what I mean by the state, because in Finland you live inside four states at once and it takes a foreigner, or a capuchin, to see the layers.

There is Finland, the physical state: the lake, the forest, the roads, the municipal taxes, the buses. It does not know it contains a tropical superpower. When the municipal council writes minutes, Bannanivaltio does not appear in them. When the city plows the road near greenhouse 3 it is not helping Bannanivaltio, it is doing its duty; the difference does not exist on paper.

Then there is Bannanivaltio, the papered state: three hundred hectares of glass, one data centre, a wind farm, an audit office with the acronym BANA, a parliament that meets in September and a constitution whose second section forbids eating future bananas, which is the one sentence in the entire national canon on which no one has ever written a report.

Then there is the association state: the Puijo Capuchin Society, a registered voluntary association that owns seven seats on the sports facility and ski trail board, all four kilos of lingonberry money in the municipal register, one hundred per cent of the national real consumption of bananas, and one rule with which I have come to agree: what is eaten is eaten.

And then there is the report state, which is not the paper state. The report state has no territory and no citizens. It exists where the documents do: in Brussels, where a standing Committee on Banana Policy declares twice a year that the situation is unchanged; in Stockholm, where the customs office of the Kingdom of Sweden holds a pledge of forty per cent of a banana export that has never existed; in Oulu, in Nebraska, in a server in Germany that keeps accounts for an audit agency that audits itself; and in a blue notebook that I carry, that carried the temperature of thirty-two sample points, and that now carries a note about a leaf.

In March 2034 the report state had the following public figures. National debt: 2.4 billion Banana Units, defined as the value of future production. Units in physical existence: two. National banana security index: 0.002 bananas per resident per year, described by the Ministry of Finance as "the first slice." Banana consumption by citizens: the capuchins. Data on what was eaten: one entry. That entry was written by the capuchins.

The audit office BANA was created in 2026, before the state, to answer the question of whether any of this was necessary. Its answer, also from 2026, was that no one could establish necessity but that the state should apply exactly the same rules as everyone else, and that BANA would review itself. The review of the self-review is ongoing.

The Ministry of the Future had in 2033 ordered a study on the harvest protocol for bunch 1/2033, the growing tip of a banana plant in row 14 of greenhouse 3. The study was assigned to an office that did not have a banana. The bunch that the study was about died before it ripened. This was investigated by a customs officer named A. Strömberg from Haparanda, and I will come to that later — it is not a small thing, more than one person is still involved.

For now, the state I served was the one that, on 8 November 2034, recorded in the ledger of the Frost Maintenance Office a note about row 14:

"Plant A, row 14: new flower spike. Estimated ripeness: none. Status: not yet a bunch. Note: bunch 1/2033, deceased, removed from the national balance sheet as the first asset line ever to correspond to reality."

I copied that note into my blue notebook, because it is a good note, and because it says a thing about my country that my country does not yet know. The only line in the balance sheet that corresponds to reality is the line for something that died. Everything else is a promise of leaves.
