# Part Two: The Warm Moss

## Chapter 4: North Gate

Every state has a mascot, and Bannanivaltio's mascot stands at the north gate of the greenhouse and says nothing. It was built in the first winter by somebody in the maintenance workshop — the records do not say who, because a state that records everything has never learned to ask its own workers themselves — and it has been rebuilt every winter since, at the same spot, always facing greenhouse 3. It is a snowman.

I knew all this about the snowman in April 2034, because Aila told me on my first spring walk. What the record does not tell you, and what nobody told me, is that a snowman that is rebuilt every winter at the same spot, on a spot where the glass is warm, becomes an instrument.

By the end of April the snowman was a metre of grey slush with a plastic bucket hat, and the ground under it had turned into a patch of open soil that never froze, extending about a metre from the glass and about ten centimetres away from it. Where does the warm soil stop. That is the information in the patch. Aila measured it every second Friday. I carried the probe while she wrote.

At the south gate the soil was frozen to the glass. At the east, the same. At the north gate, the soil was soft, and in the soft soil something was growing that had no business growing in Savo, a pale green nothing, a shoot the size of a thumbnail.

"Frost man," Aila said, and pointed with her pen. "Write that down as a non-conformity."

I wrote: "13.4. Shoot observed near north gate, ambient +2°C. Plant of origin unknown. Not recorded in the national accounts."

The north gate is not the public gate. The public gate is at the south, where the visitors come, and the visitors come mainly in the summer, in the thousands, to see the tropics of an interior country. Their bus stops, they walk the walkway between the glass piles and the mist, they take photographs of a banana bunch hanging eleven metres above water, and they go home. In the state accounts this is called tourism and carries a 1.9 percent share of GDP-B, and GDP-B, the modified national product of Bannanivaltio, includes consultancy reports in the denominator. I'll get to that.

At the north gate there is no walkway. There is a gravel road, a compressor unit, a rock like a sleeping elk, and the snowman's patch of warm mud. And there is a gate in the glass with a two-stage airlock that responds to the pass and not to the hand, which means it exists for the workers and not for the tourists, and which every evening of every winter smells, for about ninety seconds, like the south.

I had been on the job six weeks when the pass stopped working at the north gate.

I did what you do. I went back to the office. Ilmari was out — he had been out more and more, his knees pacing him. His replacement, when I found him, was a young man from the Ministry of the Future on a rotation that the job description called "operational training but strategic." His name was Timo, and he printed me a new pass.

"That gate has been closed," he said.

"Since when?"

"From the report of the standing committee."

"The standing committee closed a gate?"

He looked at me with the expression of a man who has realised that this, in fact, is his job eight hours a day.

"The standing committee closed a gate," he said. "Hintaa."

He meant "it's cheaper." But that is not what the Finnish expression means. It means a state that earns its money by being cold cannot afford to be warm at the edges.

But we are a state of the edge. So it's better you know this now: at Bannanivaltio, everything is a marginal cost except the people. The people are the part of the budget that cannot be reduced.

## Chapter 5: The Elder Speaks

In April the capuchin society held its spring meeting at Effluence Pipe 3, which is its winter address and also its legal address, and the meeting lasted twenty minutes and I was invited by accident.

I should explain. I was not invited. There was a list called "parties wishing to be heard," and somebody had typed the phrase "frost man," and the chairman of the society had heard that there was a frost man, and had approved the addition. So a registered voluntary association of apes had, without knowing it, extended a hearing to a state employee on the strength of a typo.

The chairman introduced herself as T. Kapusiini, no relation. I have to explain that. There are now about thirty free-living capuchins on Puijo and one in the audit office, and they distinguish themselves by initial, as humans do, and T. Kapusiini was T.

The society's accountant was also present, sipping a lime juice from a plastic bottle, a member of the society from the old days, the one who had, in the words of the state's environmental report, "escaped to Puijo in the same spring the greenhouse was completed."

Somebody asked him once how long he had been out. He said, "Since there was a door."

The items of that year's spring meeting were, as usual, two lines. There is no point in quoting the whole thing. Item 1 was four kilos of lingonberries. Item 2 was a bunch.

"Item two: the bunch in greenhouse 3, row 14," T. Kapusiini said. "It is fourteen pendulum fingers. It has been growing since the April of last year. The audit office's estimate for ripening is the second of January. The society wishes to make a comment for the record."

She paused for a moment, and then said, not to me but to the pipe: "The bunch will be ripe before the first of November. We have paid for the pipe four winters. We record what is eaten, and what is eaten is eaten. The state records what is expected. The difference is the whole country."

Nobody spoke. There is a way in which banal facts fall in a room where everyone is a monkey, and the fact of the twenty-seventh of October fell in the room like a rock into water. A monkey at the back, holding a half-peeled banana, said nothing and looked at the others, and the others looked down.

I said, "The audit office's estimate says January."

Then the elder put down the lime juice and cleared his throat, and addressed me for the one and only time in my life, and I have come to think of it as the shortest instruction manual to a country ever delivered.

"Akseli," he said — because even the monkeys have my name now; it is in the files; I have been observed by this state more times than I have been observed by any single human — "the state does not eat. The state reports. We are not the state. We are hungry. Do you understand?"

I said I understood, and the meeting closed fourteen minutes after it was called to order, and I carried home a note in my blue book:

04.04. Puijo Capuchin Society, spring meeting, effluence pipe 3. Statement of the chairman: bunch 1/2033 will be ripe before 1.11.2034, by the society's own count. State records say January. The difference is roughly seventy days, and it is still in the state's books.

Also: in the ledger of what the state owes, the country has, as of that Sunday evening, one asset that could not be booked, could not be pledged, could not be exported, and could not be estimated: a comment for the record, made at a pipe, by a chairman who is a monkey, about a bunch the state had counted as a promise. Nobody wrote it down. Nobody reported it. It exists. It is the first use of the word eat by a branch of government of Bannanivaltio, and it is still, as of today, standing, without an audit.
