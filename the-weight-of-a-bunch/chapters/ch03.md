# Part Three: The Receipts

## Chapter 6: Ilmari's Retirement

Ilmari Virtanen retired at the end of June, in State month Growth, at a ceremony attended by nobody, because the ceremony had been moved twice to accommodate a steering group, and by the third move there was no one left who remembered being invited. He marked the occasion, at the office, by taking the wall calendar down. Then he hung it back up. Then he took it down again and put it in his bag, and I understood that his thirty-four years of public service ended at the moment a calendar with the word Promisemonth stopped hanging on his wall.

His last professional recommendation, entered into the state file that day, was one line:

"The frost front of the state advances at the speed of its reporting. Recommend no additional reports."

The recommendation was misfiled under Weather Illegible and never surfaced again. It exists. I have it. It is the only piece of paper Ilmari ever gave me, and I keep it inside the blue notebook, because the notebook is the only file in this country where the file does not need to know that it is a file.

The posting that filled his chair was split between two half-time officers, one from the Ministry of the Future and one from the audit office, each of whom spent their first week asking for a glossary of the glossary. Aila read the notebooks as before. I walked Wednesday and Friday as before. That was the year of the two half-time officers, and I will try to explain why the state of the world's largest tropical superpower was, from June to September of 2034, functionally administered by a man with a clipboard, a woman with a pen, and thirty capuchins who eat.

## Chapter 7: The Tourists and the Trucks

In July I learned about the trucks.

I had seen them, of course — everyone who has been to Mustakorpi has seen them — but I had not counted them, because counting a thing changes it, and until then they had been simply what is. On Tudpää road, which is one lane wide and seven kilometres long, sitting between the greenhouse and the Ice Lantern road, the trucks go through four hundred and twelve times a year, which is the number the capuchin society counted, and after they counted it, the number belonged to them and the road became, briefly, a measuring instrument.

At the loading dock, numbered 4, the trucks stop for about twenty minutes. They do not load bananas. They load study documents. The Port of Torneå handles, on an average summer day, four hundred tonnes of green bananas a year from an equatorial port, and it handles, in the same period, one thousand and five hundred pages of banana-potential analysis from the largest tropical superpower in Northern Europe. The pages go to Rotterdam. The Rotterdam customs office enquires whether the pages, being printed matter, fall under Harzburg class 4901, and the answer is that they do, and that they carry import duty, and that the duty is paid by the state of Bannanivaltio, from the budget line Receipts.

"Little man, what's this?" a tourist asked me once, and pointed at the loading dock.

"That's a road."

"I know it's a road."

I said, "The trucks don't carry fruit. The fruit is an estimate."

She asked me if the estimate was a kind of fruit, and I said no, and she asked what it was, and for one second I was thirty-six years old, in the sun, on a road where I could see the hill of Puijo, and I decided not to lie.

"It's a receipt," I said. "The state writes a receipt for a banana before the banana exists. Then the banana is in the books. Then the banana is in the books forever. Then the state is solvent."

She asked, "And what about the banana?"

I looked at greenhouse 3, at row 14, where the growing tip of a plant the size of a rowing boat had been pushing a bunch out into the country's imagination since June 2033.

"I'll show you," I said.

We walked out, she and I and her son, who was maybe six, and we did not go through the glass, because you cannot go through the glass; you look at it from outside if you are a frost man and not a ticket holder. And I pointed, and she said, "It's a flower."

And her son said, "No, it's a hand."

I wrote that night in my blue book: "A child at the east viewing point called the bunch a hand. Corrected him. Mine is the note that is incorrect."

## Chapter 8: The Warmth That Had a Cause

In October two things happened in the state, and they happened at different speeds.

The first thing happened in the newspapers, which is the slowest speed known to science. BANA published its third audit of the export programme, in which it is established for the record that 2.3 million bananas had been exported, of which 40 per cent to the Kingdom of Sweden under a bilateral pledge, and the pledge corresponds to 412 truckloads, and the trucks exist and are documented, and of the bananas themselves the audit located 1 peel, held as a sample by the capuchin society, and the audit did not consider the peel a sample of 2.3 million, and in fact considered it a different category of asset, "peel," and created the category, and booked the peel at two hundred and fifty thousand Banana Units for reasons it declined to explain.

The second thing happened on the ground, at a speed I can only describe as the speed of a stem.

It began as a paper that somebody left on my desk, which Aila had flagged because Aila reads everything, on the north-gate route. It was a satellite thermal analysis from an office in Vienna, archived under the caption Not relevant to the scope, taken on 27 October 2034 at 14:04, showing the glass wall of greenhouse 3 at a temperature of +31°C at a point where, for the four preceding years, the temperature of the glass at that point had never, on any measurement, in any month, exceeded +29.4°C.

The glass was warm. And the information had been classed as irrelevant, because glass is not a nuclear installation, and the study that produced the measurement had been engaged for another purpose entirely.

I remember standing in the archive room holding the page and thinking: this is 61 degrees north, and this is October, and the glass is warm because something inside is warm, and inside the glass is a bunch that the state believes will be ripe in January.

And at the same time Aila called me. And at the same time, at the north gate, the snowman was melting.

## Chapter 9: The Names

I must tell you about the snowman in the month of October 2034, because the capuchins will tell you, and the capuchins will tell you better, but they will not tell you the part about the carrot, and the carrot is the fact I have not yet dared to write down.

The snowman that winter was built to the same design as every winter: two tiers, a bucket hat, two eyes of coal, a carrot. There was a rule about the carrot. I never found the rule. But here is what I know from Aila: on 27 October, at around 14:00, the north-gate snowman's face began to run. At 14:15 the run had reached the collar. At 16:00 the first coal eye was floating at the collar line. On the morning of 28 October the face had moved down the snowman about a hand's width. None of this was recorded in the frost log, because the frost log records what the probe says, and the probe was pointed at the ground.

The capuchin society's ledger recorded it. One line: "27.10. Snowman crying."

The audit office's prospective ripening date was 2 January. The 2033 blossom notice, filed by the audit office, had been dated 17.06.VII — which is the state's own calendar. The archivist, converting the state date into the Gregorian month for a computer system that does not understand state months, had read the numeral VII as the seventh Gregorian month, June. In Bannanivaltio's calendar it is the seventeenth day of Establishment month, in the state's seventh year. The conversion error is fifty-six days, and for a year every projection of the bunch's ripeness was drawn from a date fifty-six days late.

Strömberg arrived on the sixth day of Promisemonth in the state's ninth year, 11 November 2034, by train, from Haparanda. He was in his fifties, with a briefcase, a thermos, and a copy of the dock book, and he is the only official in this account who has read a document belonging to this state from cover to cover. I mention that because it matters to what happened on the seventh day of November, and because the seventh day of November has no name in the state's calendar, and the state's calendar, now, is the only calendar this account will use.

I have not yet written what happened on the seventh of November. I am going to tell the story the way I walked at Mustakorpi: the warm strip first, then the snow, then the glass. Understanding arrives at the edges first, where you lose it, and only afterward in the middle, where you wanted it.

## Chapter 10: The Blue Notebook: Two Documents

Before the investigation comes to the core of the state, and before I tell you about the seventy days and the third-of-January letter, you should have the two documents that were in the possession of the State of Bannanivaltio in early November of 2034, both of which I saw.

The first is a letter, dated 27 October 2034, from the Swedish customs authority, addressed to the bunch. It reads:

"We do not oppose the harvest of the bunch or its consumption. We note that the bunch is the collateral of the Kingdom of Sweden under pledge of 2027, that the pledge was paid for in advance, and that the Kingdom has received, in exchange for its payment, 412 truckloads of documents. Should the bunch be consumed, the Kingdom requests a receipt for the consumption. Yours faithfully."

The second is a final remark from the head of the audit office, K. Kapusiini, dated 3 October 2034, under the file number 41–44, filed away in Brussels because pages 41 to 44 are the final pages of the office's annual report and the report was never read past page 40. It is one sentence and a recommendation, and I will try to reproduce it from memory, though I have it in front of me.

"The bunch should be eaten on the day it ripens. The clarification of ownership shall not delay the eating. What is eaten is eaten."

I want to be precise about the condition of Bannanivaltio in the autumn of 2034, because the story is not about a letter from Sweden, and it is not about a misfiled page, and it is not about a melting snowman, and it is not even about a banana, and I want to have said so before the events of the seventh of November.

A banana is an organic object. It has a stem, a hand, a peel, and a timing of its own. It will be ready or it will not be ready. It cannot be reported into readiness, pledged into readiness, promised into readiness, audited into readiness, measured into readiness, satellite-imaged into readiness, calendar-indexed into readiness, nor — and this is the fact at the bottom of this novel — received into readiness. Nothing becomes a banana by being awaited.

But a state is not an organic object, and Bannanivaltio had built for itself a constitution with one prohibition in it — the future banana shall not be eaten — in place of a constitution with, for example, a flag, or an anthem, or a border, or a sentence in it that says what happens when the future ripens. It had a receipt instead of a banana. It had a pledge instead of a receipt. It had 2.4 billion units of books instead of fourteen bunches of fruit, and 412 truck journeys instead of a single truck, and a Member State's customs pledgee instead of an eater.

And it had one asset under a glass roof at 61 degrees north, fourteen pendulum fingers of fruit hanging from a stem in row 14, warm in the picture of a satellite from Vienna, ripe in the count of an association of apes, and expected, on paper, everywhere, to be ready on a day that had not yet come.

Everything in this state, in the autumn of 2034, was waiting for the bunch. And the bunch was waiting for a day with no name.
