# Part Four: The Nameless Day

## Chapter 11: The Quorum

On the fourth of November, in state month Promise, day four, "Pooluspäivä" — I will keep the state's names for the days here, because part of what this story is about is what happens when a country names its days and then cannot find one — a notice came to the Frost Maintenance Office. It was one page, with a steering-group stamp, and it was addressed to "the officer responsible for the microclimates of row 14." That was me. I had become the officer responsible for the microclimates of row 14 without ever having applied for the position, the way most of us acquire the important jobs in this country.

The notice said that the harvest protocol of bunch 1/2033 awaited the independent assessment of the harvest protocol. The independent assessment of the harvest protocol had been commissioned from the Ministry of the Future on 30 October. Notice of this commission was given on 4 November. The assessment had not yet begun. The meeting to decide whether the bunch could be picked was scheduled for the day after the bunch was, in the society's count, already ripe.

I took the notice to Aila. She read it once, folded it in half, and put it in the folder that she keeps for papers that have been drawn up in good faith. The folder is a banana box from the north-gate route, and it is the fullest thing in the office, and I have never once seen her take anything out of it.

"Who is the harvest protocol for?" I asked her.

"For the pickers," she said.

"Who are the pickers?"

She looked at me kindly, the way she had looked at the moss strip in March, and said, "There are no pickers. There is a protocol. The pickers are an expected value."

The capuchin society heard about the notice within a day, because the capuchins hear about everything that has a stamp on it, and by noon on the fifth a delegation had arrived at the northern gate and was sitting on the dock, eleven of them, with faces like an audit committee that has reviewed a budget it cannot amend. T. Kapusiini did not speak for a long time. Then she said the sentence that I want to write in full, because it is the only complete transcript of the position of the citizens of Bannanivaltio in early November of 2034, and I heard it myself.

"We counted," she said. "It is 142. This morning there are 142. In the evening we count again. If it is 142 again, the country has not eaten. If it is 141, the country has eaten. If it is 0, the country has exported. We can keep count. What we cannot keep count of is the day on which the count is permitted to matter. That day is not in the calendar. Somebody has to own it."

The delegation had come with a document for me, since I was the frost man, and since I was the frost man I was the nearest thing to the outdoors. It was the society's spring statement, April 2034, on greenhouse 3, row 14. In it was a line I had not seen before, because when I had heard it spoken aloud at the pipe, I had mistaken it for rhetoric. It read:

"Item of principle. The state does not eat. The state reports. We are hungry. A bunch that is not eaten when it is ripe is, at the moment the frost touches it, not a receipt and not an asset and not a pledge and not an expectation and not an index of national preparedness. It is food. And food that is not eaten is, in this state, the only thing that arrives too late by every calendar, and the only thing that will never, on any measurement, in any month, in any report, be on time."

The elder capuchin, the one who had escaped the first spring, signed it with a paw print and the words, in careful hand: "Since there was a door."

## Chapter 12: The Seventh of November

Here is the day that has no name, and here is what I did on it, in order.

At 06:14 in the morning, an hour before the office opened, I was at dock 4. I had not planned it. I had woken in the dark, and my father had been awake too, standing at his window with his bad knees, and he had said, of nothing, "The snowman is gone," and I had driven out with the probe because when my father says a thing like that at five in the morning, he does not mean it as weather.

There was indeed no snowman. There was a lens of ice on the mud, ring-shaped, like a dish. And there were sunglasses, and there was a scarf, and there was — and this is the fact I promised you — no carrot.

I walked the thirty-two sample points of the north route on autopilot, writing nothing, which is the first dereliction of my working career. At 08:40 I was back at the dock. The capuchins were already there. Eleven of them on the warm deck, and the leader of the delegation handed me a piece of paper with the day's count on it, and the count said: 142.

Later, at 14:00, the quorum came. It came in three cars and one van, and it included the chief officer of the Ministry of Finance, and the development director of the Ministry of the Future, and the chairmen of the steering group and of the harvest project steering group — the same person — and the secretary of the monitoring group, and a local representative of the insurance company, and Don Esteban Paredes, of the Food and Agriculture Organization, who had grown bananas for forty years on the coast of Ecuador and had been at Mustakorpi since 2031 and had never once been asked anything, and it did not include anyone whose job description, in any archive in this state, contained the word pick.

They stood on the dock with the paperwork of a protocol that could not be executed, and Don Esteban Paredes stood among them, holding a thermos and a phone with a photograph of his own plants on it, and the glass of greenhouse 3 was, on that day, at +31.2°C. I was invited as the officer responsible for microclimates, which is to say I was invited as a thermometer, and thermometers do not have opinions, and for the first four hours of the meeting I behaved like one.

At 16:50 the meeting took its decision, which was:

The harvest of bunch 1/2033 shall proceed upon the completion of the independent assessment of the harvest protocol, currently commissioned, delivery expected within the regular review period.

The regular review period is fourteen months.

At 17:30 the quorum left. At 17:40 Don Esteban Paredes left, after shaking my hand and saying, in slow, careful English: "Where I come from, this is not a state. I have no word for this. Where I come from, we eat."

At 18:15 I was alone on the dock. And here I must be exact, because exactness is the one thing I can give and the one thing the papers of this state cannot.

The airlock opened. I did not have a pass for greenhouse 3. The capuchins had a pass. They have always had a pass; the society was registered in 2028 and its registration, under the associations act, carries the same access rights as any other registered association of residents of the region, and includes, in the annex nobody has ever amended, "harvest rights of one bunch per fiscal year, for the purposes of the society's statutory object, which is the nutrition of its members." I have read the annex. The annex has never been contested. It has never been tested, because no bunch has ever been harvested, but it has never been contested.

The capuchins went in ahead of me. And I want to say in my defence that I did not go in to eat. I went in because I am the frost man, and the glass was warm, and I had in my hand the only instrument I have ever trusted, and I wanted to measure, at the source, exactly one time, at the level of the bunch, what +31.2°C means for a banana that has been expected for four hundred and eleven days.

The bunch hung at the height of my own chest. Eleven pendulum fingers. Under it the floor was warm, and there was a small metal table, at the south end, on which the insurance company had left, months ago, in another meeting, a folder of compensation offers that no one had signed. The fruit had a smell, and I can't write down what the smell was like, because the words we have for smell are the words we have for things that are already past. It smelled the way a room smells when somebody has just left.

The elder came out of the row of foliage and stopped at the end of the table, and there was a moment — I have decided to report it, because this is my notebook, and in my notebook the record is not what the probe says — in which a capuchin monkey and a frost man looked at each other over fourteen fingers of fruit, at 61 degrees north, at 18:15, on a day that has no name in the calendar of the state that owes both of them a living.

He did not offer it to me. Nobody in this story is going to be offered anything. He put one hand on the stem and held the bunch still. And then he said, in the way he says things, looking at the fruit and not at me, the shortest fiscal policy of the state of Bannanivaltio, made at a table inside a greenhouse by a monkey in a sweater of waste heat:

"It is ripe."

And I took one banana out of the second hand from the stem, because the second hand from the stem is the one that comes free, and I peeled it there, standing up, still holding the probe, and I ate it.

I have read the constitution of Bannanivaltio. Section 2: no future banana shall be eaten. I can report, as the only person in the European Union who has tested the question, that the banana was not a future banana. It was present. It was there. It was ripe. It was eleven minutes out of the state's expectation, which is one minute less than the state now owes me in unpaid microclimate overtime.

It tasted like a banana.

## Chapter 13: What Is Eaten Is Eaten

At 18:31 I wrote in my blue notebook, at the dock, standing up:

"07.11. (No name.) Microclimate, greenhouse 3, row 14: +31.2°C. Bunch count: 141. One banana eaten. Recorded as consumption. By me."

That is the whole entry. It is the first use of the word consumption in the state records of Bannanivaltio for an act that the state did not itself perform. It is ten words long. It took me eleven seconds to write, and it is still, at the time of this writing, the only financial statement in the entire national account of Bannanivaltio that a capuchin has verified and a customs officer has found in order.

The capuchin society's ledger for the same night contains the figure 141, without comment.

Of the remaining 141, at the count of the society on the morning of 8 November, none were eaten. They did not eat them. I want that said, and I want it said loudly, because in November of 2034 a state produced 2.3 million units of estimated fruit and fourteen real bunches, and out of all of it the only edible things in the country were in one greenhouse, on one row, under one roof that had melted the face of a snowman, and the ones who ate were one frost man, and the ones who did not eat were the only citizens of Bannanivaltio who had voted for the right to do exactly that.

The elder sat by the bunch all night. He sat in the warm spot where the data centre's discharge pipe enters the floor, in the spot the ice never reaches, with his hand against the glass, and in the morning the bunch was soft, and in the evening, on the eighth of November, the society entered in the dock book the following entry:

"8.11.2034. Terttu. Nothing edible. Account: 141, expected; 142, promised; 0, existing."

The state entered, on the same day, under the balance sheet, a new line, and I have scanned it, and I attach it here as it is the entire civilisation of my country in four lines:

| Item | Value (BY) | Availability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bunch 1/2033, lost | 0 | Not available, as rotten |

That was the first line in the ledger of the state of Bannanivaltio that corresponded to reality. K. Kapusiini, head of the audit office, signed it with his own name, with one n.

## Chapter 14: The Investigation

A. Strömberg, customs officer of the Kingdom of Sweden, stationed at Haparanda, arrived at Puijo on 11 November 2034, under a mandate to ascertain, on behalf of the pledgee of a bunch that was, by then, not an object in any ledger of any country, what had happened to the collateral.

I carried his bags from the taxi. I want to say in advance of everything else in this chapter that I do not regret what I did, and that I would do it again, and that he knew, and that he wrote it down, and that of all the thousands of pages filed against the name of the second-greatest banana superpower in European history, his thirty-four pages are the only ones I have ever read a second time.

He interviewed the society at dock 4, because the witnesses would not leave the scene of the crime. The transcript of that interview is public, and I will not reproduce it here. I will only report the moment that matters. After four hours, Strömberg closed his notebook, and said, "I am looking for whoever killed the bunch."

And the chairman of the society said, "You are looking for everybody."

He looked at her for a long time. And then he did the thing that the Ministry of Finance has still, at the date of this writing, not acknowledged, and that the standing committee of the European Union has classified, in its list of situations of which it declines to take note, under the category of "unhelpfully precise": he took out the sheet of the state calendar for the previous fourteen days, and he struck out, one by one, with a ruler, the meetings, the steering groups, the protocol commissions, and the monitoring sessions, and at the bottom of the page he wrote the numbers. Twelve. Then he counted the days between the twenty-seventh of October and the eighth of November. Thirteen.

"The state calendar has no day with the name of the thirteenth day," he said. "On the day that has no name, something was still alive, and something was still possible, and every institution in this country had an alibi. I have investigated a pledge worth 2.4 billion units, and I find that it was killed by an appendix."

Then he did the second thing. He asked me.

He asked me, at 19:40, on the evening of the sixteenth of November, in the kitchen of the ski lodge at Puijo, whether the report of the audit of the microclimate of row 14, filed on the eighth of November by the officer responsible, was in order.

I said it was.

He drank his coffee. He looked at the window. Beyond the window, at the north gate, the sunglasses were still lying on the ice lens, nobody having collected them, because in this country an object is only evidence if a form says it is evidence, and there is no form for lost sunglasses of a snowman.

"And the count?" he said. "One day earlier, one day later?"

I looked at him. He looked at me. And this is the part of the story of my country that I have not told to the tax office, to the audit office, to the standing committee, to the pledgee, or to my father, and I am telling it now, at the end of a book, in a language the state of Bannanivaltio does not conduct business in, because it is the only place left where the sentence has not yet been filed.

"The count was 141," I said.

Strömberg wrote it down. Nobody in the history of the art of investigation ever wrote down a number more carefully. He underlined it, he ruled a column for it, he entered it twice, and then he looked at the number, and then he looked at me, and I could see arriving on his face, in a train of thought moving at the speed of a stem, the full weight of what one banana, eaten at 61 degrees north, out of an expected value of 142, does to the arithmetic of a state.

He put his pen down.

"The books of this country," he said, "have only ever balanced twice. The first time was in 2028, when the audit office found that the association's accounts consisted of four kilos of lingonberries, and the auditors could not determine what a lingonberry is worth, and resolved the problem by reporting that the account was in a macroeconomic category of its own. The second time is tonight."

He picked up his briefcase, and stopped at the door, and said the sentence that I have put at the beginning of this book:

"In this country, what is eaten is not yet eaten until the state has reported it. And what the state has reported as eaten has not been eaten by anyone. That is the whole constitution. You ate a banana. Nobody has ever done that here before. Good night."

## Chapter 15: The Son

On the Monday of the following week the frost front moved down from Puijo to the shore of the lake, and at six in the morning the light came back over the water as it does at the end of January or the beginning of November, and my father called me and said that his knees had a forecast.

I drove to Mustakorpi one last time, and I walked the north route, and I counted: three capuchins at the warm pipe, one at the dock, one asleep in the compressor shelter, two on the roof of greenhouse 3 in the mist at the top of the glass and in the open air of Savo. At the north gate there was a mound where the snowman had been, and a small carrot set upright on the ice lens, nobody having eaten it, because a carrot that has been put out as an instrument is not food and cannot be eaten; it is a datum. I know how that reads. It is what this country taught me.

At the end of the north route Aila was waiting for me at the gate of greenhouse 3 with her pen, and she did not write a note in the ledger and she did not fill in a form. She told me that the audit office had, that morning, closed the file on bunch 1/2033 with the following closing remark, one line, final:

"The bunch ripened. The bunch rotted. The counting continues."

And then she told me the second thing, and I am going to end this book on it, and I would like it to be understood that nearly everything I have written in this novel is a state document of a kind, and that the following is the one thing in it that is true in the way that a state is not.

At the root of the plant that had carried bunch 1/2033, at the base of the row, in the warm soil of the discharge pipe, there was a green shoot the height of a hand. A banana plant fruits once. When it is done, it dies at the root, and out of the root, from the same old stem that made it, a new plant comes up. The agronomists, who have known this for four thousand years, have a word for it. Don Esteban Paredes, who is from Ecuador, said it to me in the greenhouse in Spanish, and I no longer have the translation pending from the Ministry of the Future, because I asked him myself, in the spring, after the shoot was up, when I had some days and he had some time.

El hijo. The son.

I asked Aila to enter it in the ledger.

"Under which item?" she said.

"Under plant. New."

"Spontaneous?"

I thought about that. Spontaneous is the word for a thing that happens to nobody, out of nothing, without a form. The state's books are full of spontaneous items: the spontaneous value of a pledge, the spontaneous growth of a study, the spontaneous continuation of a meeting. The books contain entire ministries of the spontaneous. What they do not contain, and what they have never contained, and what they will not contain until someone at the bottom of the frost scale, at the edge of the warm strip, with a blue notebook and no opinions and a probe, writes a line that costs the state nothing and cannot be pledged and cannot be audited and cannot be exported and cannot be waited for, is an item that is simply real.

"Item," I said. "One."

Aila wrote it down. She did not ask for the number of the row. There is only one row at Mustakorpi that anyone has ever counted.

That evening I went home to Neulamäki. My father was at the window, at his mark, at "ammunition." I put a pot with a little savo earth in it on the sill, and in the pot I put a banana seed, which I had taken from the last ripe banana of the state of Bannanivaltio, and I do not have a form for it, and I did not ask.

People in this country sometimes ask me, when they hear that I worked at Mustakorpi, whether bananas really grow here. I tell them the truth, which is that an entire government of the European Union reports every year on bananas grown at 61 degrees north, and that the total amount ever eaten is one, and that the total amount ever reported is 2.3 million, and that the difference between those two numbers is the gross domestic product of the country where I was born.

And then I tell them about the shoot. It is up to about a knee now. It is in a pot on a windowsill at Neulamäki, in front of a pencil mark that says ammunition. On the day of this writing it has one leaf, and the cat has ignored it twice, and my father, who is seventy-two and does not believe in the greenhouse, has watered it eleven times without being asked.

Nobody reports the watering. Nobody books it. Nobody files it under a category, exports it, pledges it, satellites it or audits it.

I have not counted it. If I counted it, it would become a number, and the state would pay for it, and the account would be in the books, and the books of the state of Bannanivaltio are not a place where a thing this small would survive the winter.

But I want it on the record, after all, and this is the only ledger I trust:

Son. One. Watered.

What is eaten is eaten. What is grown is grown.
