## Chapter: The Girl Who Wishes to Be Heard

### The Neighbour

The reason I know the word for what I am, in the eyes of the state of Bannanivaltio — the word being "stakeholder" — is a girl in Neulamäki called Anniina Virta, who is eleven, and who in the spring of 2034 put a slip of paper through the letter slot of my father's house.

The paper was a notice. The notice had been generated by a system. Under the stamp of the Ministry of the Future it read:

"TO: RESIDENT. RE: GREENHOUSE 3, ROW 14, BUNCH 1/2033. YOU ARE A STAKEHOLDER. YOU HAVE THE RIGHT TO BE HEARD. IF YOU WISH TO BE HEARD, YOU MUST REGISTER."

Anniina had distributed, as she told my father with the shining accuracy of a child who has found the one loophole that the adults cannot close, 39 of these notices along Mustakorventie and Neulamäentie, on the grounds that a Ministry that invites residents to be heard could not in law exclude the children of the road, since the road is a shared asset, and the child had been on the road, and therefore the child was on the asset, and therefore had the standing of anything else standing on it.

She was, of course, correct. The ministry had not considered her correct, because it had not considered her at all, but she sent the argument as a letter, in school exercise book cursive, and somebody at the ministry, out of six hundred and eleven such letters that spring, had read hers to the end, and had forwarded it to the region's stakeholder register, where it was placed in the category "Domestic fauna and other warmth."

I went to her house, because I am the frost man and the notice had invited objections about microclimates, and I found a girl doing homework on a kitchen table with a radio on, and on the radio was a programme about the economic situation, and the situation, as far as I could tell, was that the economy of the state of Bannanivaltio had, in the first quarter of 2034, grown by 6.2 percent, and the cause of the growth was the reports about the growth.

"Do you want to be heard?" I asked her.

"Already have been," she said. "It took three weeks. They wrote back."

She fetched the reply. It was a letter with a reference number, and it consisted of a sentence of welcome and then a paragraph that began, "In accordance with the second section of the constitution," and it ended with the sentence that I want you to see, because the constitution, the entire constitution of Bannanivaltio, has one operative clause, and here it is, having survived nine years and reached a kitchen table in Neulamäki:

"No future banana shall be eaten. No future banana shall be budgeted. No future banana shall be presumed to exist without a forecast audit."

"What does it mean?" Anniina asked me.

And I said, "It means that the country is not allowed to eat what it has not yet grown."

She looked at the letter for a while. Then she put it in a plastic folder, because she keeps everything, this child, in plastic folders, in the belief — which I have since had occasion to confirm — that paper is the only thing in this country that does not rot.

"And the real ones?" she said. "The ones that are already growing. In the greenhouse. Can you eat those?"

I told her the truth, which is that nobody knew, because nobody had ever eaten one, and that the constitutional prohibition was about the future, and the greenhouse was technically the present, but that the greenhouse was, in the books of the audit office, a forecast, and that a banana inside a forecast is, for the purposes of a lie, not food.

She wrote that down. I have to tell you that she wrote it down in a notebook with a blue cover, which she had bought because she had seen mine, and that the words she wrote were:

"07.03. Akseli says a banana in a forecast is not food. Asked him what happens when the forecast gets old. He said the tree doesn't know about the forecast."

Then she asked me the question that I want to write here, near the front of this book, because everything afterward happened in its shadow, and because the child asked it before the Swede did, before the audit office did, before Brussels did, before Vienna did, before the satellite did, and before I did, in the order in which things are asked in a country with four states and one autumn:

"If they don't eat them," she said, "and they don't sell them, and they don't pick them — what are they for?"

I have worked in public administration. I know what an unanswerable question costs its asker. Nobody at Mustakorpi has ever answered her. The Ministry of the Future sent her a study. It was 84 pages, with a summary of 41 pages, and in the summary there is a figure, 1.9 percent, and a term, "cultural ecosystem service," and the conclusion that the bunch in row 14 contributed, in the state's account, to the identity of the region. The identity of the region. The child had asked what the banana was for and the state's answer had been the word identity, which is a word a country uses when it wants to keep the thing and not use it, the way you keep a grandfather's knife in a drawer.

### The Pencil Mark

That spring I spent several evenings at my father's house, and because he is seventy-two and cannot walk far, he walks by looking, and from his window he can see the glow of the data centre above the spruce line, and he can see the top of Puijo, and in the winter he could see, at the north gate of the greenhouse, a white point that he said was a snowman and that I, from Neulamäki, five kilometres out, could not confirm.

He told me things about the land there from before the Puijo ski trails were machine-groomed, from before the sawmill by the southern shore, from before the sawmill was the data centre and the data centre was the heat and the heat was the greenhouse and the greenhouse was a superpower. In 1971, he says, the field at Mustakorpi grew barley that came up late, and the willow warblers in the ditches started at 03:40 and went on until it was not clear whether they were birds or the sound of the barley growing. He remembers a hard frost in June of that year that took the whole of the lower field. He does not remember it as a disaster. He remembers it as the year the family on the next farm dripped candle wax into each other's ears, which is what people did then for an earache, and the wax does not come out, and a man in that family still, today, cannot hear the birds at all.

I am not sure what to do with this material in a book about a state, but a book has to be from somewhere, and this is where the book is from.

My father himself will not go into the greenhouse. He has been invited, in the way of our region, seven or eight times — the tourism office sends him a card every spring — and he has not gone, and the stated reason is his knees. I think the real reason is that my father, who is the last person in our family to have been, in any sense, a farmer, does not want to stand at +28°C under a glass roof and watch a fruit the size of a rowing boat ripen in a country that has not picked it. I have never said this to him. It is the kind of thing that a man says to a window.

But he watches the road at the north gate in the winter, from the kitchen, with the binoculars, when the dock lorries come through, and once, in February, he counted — my father, who is not a stakeholder and has never registered as one — 412 lorries, and he put the number in the margin of his Aapeli, next to the story about the barber, and he said nothing about it for two months, and when I found the number and asked him about it he said:

"That road is not carrying anything. It's carrying being about."

### Two Spellings

It is around this time, in the spring of 2034, that I learned the most useful fact in this book, which I have held back until now.

In all the documents of the state, the country's name is spelled with two n's: Bannanivaltio. In all the documents of the association, it is spelled with one: Bananivaltio. The difference, according to the capuchin society, is not a difference of spelling but a difference of what the letters are expected to do.

The state documents say B-A-N-N-A-N-I-V-A-L-T-I-O. The association's documents say B-A-N-A-N-I-V-A-L-T-I-O.

The association checked the matter with the banana itself.

What the banana says, according to the elder capuchin, is not a spelling but a frequency. A banana has a certain number of a's in it, and the number depends on how many people have already eaten from it. The state, which counts a banana before it exists, has to double the a, because in a forecast a fruit that has not been eaten needs two of everything: two syllables, two peels, two markets, two pledges. The association, which counts a banana after it has been eaten, has one banana, and therefore one a, and one n, since the n is the sound the country makes when the eating is already finished.

I do not know if this is true. I have written it down twice and both times it has come out the same, which in this country is the closest thing to verification there is. But I noticed, in November, when the audit office entered into its balance sheet the first line that corresponded to reality — bunch 1/2033, lost, 0, not available, as rotten — that the line was signed in the name of the audit office with one n. And the documents of the state, which define everything, which pledge and measure and export and audit everything, were unable that month to produce a single document in which the name of the second-greatest tropical superpower in the European Union was spelled in a way that had been checked with the fruit.
