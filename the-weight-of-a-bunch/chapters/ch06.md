## Chapter: The Counting

The dock book exists because the trucks exist, and the trucks exist because the export exists, and the export exists because the Ministry of Finance needed an export, and the export exists the way that most things exist in this country: on paper, at a rate of 412 movements a year, at the loading dock numbered 4.

The capuchin society began counting the movements in 2030. The record of the counting is now called the dock book, and the dock book is kept in a waterproof notebook on a nail inside the shelter of the effluence pipe, and it is, at the date of this writing, the longest continuously maintained instrument of observation in the state of Bannanivaltio, and its columns are these: date, lorries, bananas, remarks.

In August of 2034 I asked the chairman if I could copy a page of it, for the frost file, and she handed me the notebook, and I sat on the dock and copied a page, and then a second page, and then, because the sun was out and the deck is warm — I mean, the sun was out and the deck is warm — I copied eleven pages, and that is how the state of Bannanivaltio acquired, in the file of its frost-maintenance office, the only copy of eleven pages of the dock book that is not in the possession of the capuchins, the Swedish customs, or the audit office.

I reproduce here, in order and in full, the observations for the first week of March of 2030, because they are the oldest pages I have, and because they contain, in remarks of one column, the entire trade policy of the country:

| Day | Lorries | Bananas | Remarks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3.3.2030 | 2 | 0 | Driver ate a sandwich. Capuchins did not get one. |
| 4.3.2030 | 2 | 0 | Same driver. Same sandwich? |
| 11.5.2030 | 3 | 0 | One truck returned from Torneå. On the back: a study. |
| 17.8.2030 | 1 | 1 | Peel. From the Prime Minister's residence. Booked as export. |
| 2.11.2030 | 2 | 0 | On the side of the truck: MAKE FINLAND TROPICAL AGAIN. |

And then the annual line, which I have held back until now, and which is the whole dock book in ten words:

"Total for 2030. Lorries: 412. Bananas: 1. Note: peel."

The official export statistics of the state of Bannanivaltio for the same year record 2.3 million bananas, of which 40 per cent to the Kingdom of Sweden.

I asked the chairman how the difference is to be understood. She was silent for a moment, in the way of a person who is deciding how much of an answer the listener has earned, and then she said: "The difference is the part that hasn't ripened."

But she did not stop there, and I am going to write down the rest of it, because a dock at six in the morning in October, at 61 degrees north, is a place where a person can hear a whole country explained in the time it takes the sun to clear a spruce line.

"You have to understand," she said, "that the books of the state are not wrong. That is the terrible part. Bananas are produced. They are entered in the books as produced. Then, by a rule that no one in this state has ever had the courage to repeal, the produced bananas are transferred from domestic consumption to export. Not physically. On paper. As expectations. The expectations are pledged as security to the Kingdom of Sweden, which the pledge requires, and the trucks drive to Torneå empty, because you can't load an expectation, and they come back full, because in Torneå they load them with studies. And every month the audit office reconciles the books, and the books balance, and the balance is achieved by the only instrument the state possesses, which is the transfer of fruit from the present into the future."

"And the future never arrives," I said.

"The future is not late," the chairman said. "The future is on time. It's the country that is early. The country has spent the future. It spent it in 2027, in a pledge. It is now living forward of its own harvest, the way a drunk man lives forward of his own salary, and every autumn — every October, mark this, frost man — the debt falls due, and the debt is due on the day of the year when a banana is at its most valuable and least available, and the day is called October, and it is called October in the Gregorian calendar, which is the calendar of everybody who is owed a banana, and it is not called anything at all in the state's calendar, because the state's calendar, on the day of October when fruit falls due, has no name, and a day with no name cannot be booked."

I asked her what the society does about it.

"We count," she said. "It is the whole of our autumn programme. We are very good at it. We have four years of continuity, a statutory quorum, and a chairman. We are the only functioning institution in this state in which the number 1 means one."

## Chapter: Brussels, Twice a Year

In April of 2031, before my time, the standing advisory committee on banana policy of the European Commission met in Brussels, and the minutes of that meeting have one item that the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Bannanivaltio has, at the date of this writing, been unable to answer, and that I am going to tell you now because I heard about it in the interval between the two events of this book, at the office, on a Thursday, from Aila Palojärvi, who had a copy of the minutes in the banana box that is her archive.

The delegation of Bannanivaltio attended with, for the first time, an observer from the Puijo Capuchin Society, whose travel was paid from the budget line "Nutritional support for elite athletics," since no other budget line in the state had room. The observer sat at the back. And the minutes of item 7 of that meeting record the following:

"Marked: that in the back row of the delegation sat two persons who were not introduced and were not found on the attendance list. They were entered in the minutes under initials V. and K. During item 4, V. said: 'Unchanged.' K. replied: 'Yes. But unchanged for whom?' The replies were entered in the minutes as annex 2, because the committee records everything said in its presence."

Aila showed me the photocopy. It is the committee's own record. It has a stamp. I asked her who V. and K. were and she said that no one in the room had recognised them, and that no institution of the European Union had ever claimed them, and that their travel expenses had never been sought from any line item of any budget, and that the words stand in the committee's permanent record, twice a year, and that the committee has been collecting them since 2031.

"Unchanged," I said. "Unchanged for whom."

The second occasion concerns my own time, and I was not there either, and it reached me as a photocopy, in the post, from Brussels, addressed to "the frost man." It was sent by a person I have never met, an archivist at the standing committee called Dupré, who had, in the autumn of 2034, been organising the same cabinet for eleven years, and had in that autumn found a folder.

His covering letter is two sentences long and I quote it in full, because it is the only piece of correspondence I have received in my working life from the outside world about my own country that I have kept:

"Monsieur, I have been arranging the archive. I found a folder that nobody has ever asked for. Its name is Bannanivaltio — one n. Enclosed: four pages, numbered 41 to 44."

The four pages were the final remark of the head of the audit office, K. Kapusiini, on the subject of bunch 1/2033, dated the third of October of 2034, nine days before the bunch was dead. I have described the document already: the bunch should be eaten on the day it ripens; the clarification of ownership shall not delay the eating; what is eaten is eaten.

It had been sent to the standing committee as an attachment to the minutes of the meeting of the sixth of November, and the committee had found the situation unchanged and had archived the attachment without opening the folder, and the folder had been filed under the spelling on its own cover, which was the association's spelling, which is to say not the state's spelling, and so it was filed in the place where files of the association would go, which is a place that nobody in the state of Bannanivaltio has ever, in the eleven-year history of the standing committee, had occasion to search.

Dupré transferred it. He said, in a later letter, which I also keep, that he had not moved the folder because it was misfiled, and he had not moved it because it was correct. He said he had made a copy of it, and put the copy next to the original, in a cabinet with two labels, and that the labels were these:

Bannanivaltio — the state's spelling.
Bananivaltio — the other thing.

"There are two cabinets in my archive," he wrote. "Both are correct. Both now contain this country. It is, I think, the only state in the Union that can be lost and found by spelling."

## Chapter: The Weather

On the fifteenth of November 2034, at seven in the morning, the radio in my father's kitchen said a name I had not heard before, and the name was Marisol, and by nine it was the only word in the kitchen.

A hurricane had made landfall on the northern coast of Honduras. The valley called Sula — I had never heard of it either; my geography, like the state's, runs north to south and then stops — was underwater. Two-thirds of the plantations. The dead, in the first broadcast, were counted in the dozens, and by the evening bulletins in the hundreds, and nobody in the country where I was born was being slowly counted in either of those years, and I want that to be said plainly, because thereafter, in every room in this story, including this one, the arithmetic of Honduras is the largest number in the room, and the number we were guarding, 142, is not.

Don Esteban Paredes came to our kitchen at Neulamäki for the first time that morning. He had his own coast — El Oro, a different country, a different coastline, four hundred kilometres and an entire lifetime of growing away from the Sula valley — and he had made a call, and the call had lasted a long time, and he had spoken quietly, and then he had sat at our table with a coffee he did not drink.

My father put out bread. Neither of them ate much.

"Where I come from," Paredes said at last, to the window, in his slow careful English, "when the fruit is about to be ready, we work the night. We sleep in the field. There is no study, no permit, no assessment of harvest protocol. You are wet, it is four in the morning, everybody is there. The fruit cannot wait for the daylight and it cannot wait for a form. This is a thing that a country knows or does not know. I have worked in the fruit forty years. It is not knowledge. It is the way a country breathes."

At nine my father, who is seventy-two and has heard every possible weather in this region for a lifetime, said the thing that I have come to consider the man's whole career in one sentence, and it was said to the radio, in the flat tone of a person stating a measurement:

"Somebody should tell them the bananas are ready."

Nobody at the table answered him, because nobody at the table had a ministry, or a pledge, or a quorum, or a satellite, or a budget line, or a calendar with a name for the day. But there was bread on the table, and there was coffee, and the bread got eaten, and the coffee got drunk, and what is eaten is eaten, and I write it here because in the state where I work, in this month, in this year, that was the only sentence of fiscal policy that anyone in the room was able to carry out.

The insurance man came up from Kuopio the next day. Mr. Hargreaves, of London, grey coat, wellingtons, a leather notebook older than the state. He had taken the weather satellite graphs from the bottom of the file and the refrigerator of the ski lodge and laid them out on both, side by side, and he showed me the two lines. A thousand-metre pixel, from Vienna, of warm glass. A ruler, from Haparanda, of a puddle.

"Two instruments," he said. "Same wall, same date. One says warming. One says weather. In your state there is a twelve-year-old institution whose entire method is to decide which instrument to file under which heading. Between us, Mr. Hämäläinen, I have counted the days. Thirteen of them. In the thirteenth day, the thirteenth meeting, the thirteenth signature, an event we classify in my business as force majeure — the weather arrived and it was too late."

"Too late for what?" I asked.

He looked at the graphs. He looked at the window, past the window, up the direction of the hill.

"Too late for everything that the weather was going to be too late for," he said. "In my industry we never say the sentence plainly, but you seem the type. In my industry, the loss that could not be foreseen is the loss that is the biggest. It is the only loss that pays out. Everything else is what my office calls an operating cost of optimism."

I want to be exact: he did not say what the optimism was operating. I put the question to him at the door, and he considered it, this man who was the only adult male in a hundred kilometres outside the banana region of Savo who knew what a bunch is worth at each of its stages, and he answered me in the jargon of his profession, which is one word, and I write it here because it is correct:

"Hope."
