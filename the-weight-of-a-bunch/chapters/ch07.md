# Part Five: The Winter Account

## Chapter: The Field With No Box

The form arrived on the first working day of December, and it was, I want to be fair to it, a form of unusual beauty. It had been designed by a working group in the Ministry of Finance over a period of eleven weeks, and it had a header, a reference, a legal basis, three copies, and a colour, which was blue, and it was addressed to me.

The heading of the form read: NOTIFICATION OF CONSUMPTION OF A BANANA OF THE STATE, PURSUANT TO SECTION 2 OF THE CONSTITUTION AND TO DECISION BANA-KKO/2034/1.

I would like you to imagine holding this form. It is four pages. Page one asks for the identity of the consumer. Page two asks for the identity of the banana, by row, by hand, and by pendulum finger. Page three asks for the date of consumption, and offers, as a dropdown in the electronic version, a list of the twelve named days of a month of the state calendar. Page four asks for the value of the banana consumed, in Banana Units, to be entered by the consumer.

I filled in pages one and two at my desk, at the Frost Maintenance Office, on the fourth of December, in state month Reporting. On page one I wrote my name, my personnel number, and, under "capacity in which consumed," the phrase "as a private person," which the field would not accept, and then "other," which it would, and then, in the free-text field that "other" opens, the word "hunger," which the field also accepted, because a text field will accept anything, and that, in the end, is the whole difference between a person and an institution.

On page three the dropdown did not contain the seventh of November. The state's calendar has names for its days — Initiative Day, Preparation Day, Coordination Day, Stakeholder Day, Comment Day, Steering Group Day, Interim Report Day, Impact Day, Continuation Funding Day, Establishment Day — and for five unnamed days at the turn of the year, and none of the twenty-four offers matched. I wrote "the day that has no name," and the electronic system rejected it, and the paper system, which is the one in the regional offices, accepted it, because the paper system is read by clerks and the electronic system is read by a parser.

Page four I left empty for three days.

I want to explain those three days, because page four is where the constitution of my country actually lives. Section 2 forbids the eating of future bananas. A banana that has been eaten is, by definition, not a future banana, and so the eating was, in the strict reading, permitted — but the books of the state contained no category for it. The state had categories for pledged bananas, expected bananas, estimated bananas, exported bananas, bananas in transit, bananas in storage, bananas in audit, bananas in study, and bananas lost to rot. The one category it had never needed was eaten, and the reason it had never needed it is the reason for this book: a state that has spent its future in advance has no inventory, and a state with no inventory has nothing that can be eaten.

On the third day Aila Palojärvi came to my desk, and looked at the blank page four, and took the pen out of my hand.

"You are not the value," she said. "You're the event. The value is the state's."

She filled in page four herself, in her own handwriting, which is the only handwriting in the office that the audit office accepts on sight, and the figure she wrote was:

One banana. Market value, at the world price prevailing on the day of consumption plus the hurricane adjustment: 0.0000004 BY. Remark: not priced. Livelihood of the person consuming: unchanged. Net effect on national accounts: the state had one fewer banana than it believed, and one more fact than it has ever had before.

The form went to the audit office, and the audit office, which audits itself, took the unusual step of auditing the form rather than the person, and produced, on the eighteenth of December, a decision that I have read perhaps forty times since, and that I set down here from the file copy:

"Notification ACK/2034/1 reports the first verified consumption of a banana of the State of Bannanivaltio in the history of the state. The Office notes that the verified figure is smaller than the smallest figure previously carried in the accounts by a factor of 5.7 billion. The Office further notes that it is the only figure in the national accounts that has been verified by two independent instruments, namely: the person who ate it, and the association that counted to 141. The Office finds that it has no category for this figure and creates one. The category is: EATEN. The figure is entered under EATEN and nowhere else. No future banana is affected. Closed."

K. Kapusiini signed the decision with one n.

## Chapter: The Working Group on the Name

In January the Ministry of the Future convened the working group on the naming of the unnamed day, and I was appointed to it as the stakeholder-side expert on microclimates, which made me the only member of the working group who had been outside in November.

There were nine of us. There was a chair from the Ministry of the Future, a jurist from the Ministry of Justice, a statistician from the audit office, a representative of the Kingdom of Sweden by video link, a representative of the standing committee by video link, a calendar historian from the university, who had prepared a paper on intercalary days in ecclesiastical calendars going back to the Council of Nicaea, and three of us from the region.

The charta of the working group was one sentence: "The working group shall propose a name for the day of the year which the calendar, in years divisible by four, does not name, taking into account the constitutional, statistical and international implications of naming."

We met four times. I have the minutes. I will not give them in full. But I want the record of the third meeting to be in this book, because at that meeting the calendar historian — a small, quick man named Rautio who had spent his whole career on the leap day and had never once been asked about it by anyone in power — said the thing that eventually broke the working group, and I heard it myself.

"Gentlemen," he said, "every calendar in the world that has an unnamed day has had one of two reasons. Either the day is unlucky, and it is left outside the count so that the count stays clean — the Roman February had a hole in it like that, and the hole was where the dead lived. Or the day belongs to somebody who has not been invited to the naming, and the naming waits for them. I have read the file of this case. The bunch was ripe on that day. The snowman was crying on that day. One banana was consumed on that day, pursuant to notification ACK/2034/1, category EATEN. You are not trying to name a hole in your calendar. You are trying to name the day on which your country first actually happened. I recommend you let the people who were present name it."

The ministry draft, which was circulated before the vote, proposed "Continuity Day," on the grounds that the day fell in the interval between years and thus secured the continuity of the calendar. The statistician of the audit office — who had entered the figure 141 into the national accounts — proposed "Verified Day." The jurist proposed, with magnificent caution, "the Day of Closing."

The representative of the society, who attended the fourth meeting and who is, whatever else may be said of this country, its best parliamentary speaker, was given five minutes, and used three.

"The state does not need a name for that day," she said. "The state needs a name for what happened on it, and that is what the state cannot say. I will tell you what happened on it. On that day the country's books were accurate for one banana. A man ate it. Then the country had, for one day, exactly as many bananas as it actually had, and the number was one fewer than the day before, and every institution of this state has been trying for two months to make that fact smaller. You will not succeed. One is the smallest number there is. You cannot round it. Name the day after the count. Call it the Count, and every year on the day without a name, the country counts, and the count is true. It's the only day when it is."

The working group voted. Continuity Day, two votes. Verified Day, two votes. The Day of Closing, one vote. "The Count," three votes. Abstentions, one, that being the representative of the Kingdom of Sweden, who said that his office was obliged to treat any day on which the collateral of his kingdom was consumed as a working day and could not in law also celebrate it.

The recommendation went to the ministry in February. The ministry, in March, returned it to the working group for an assessment of the constitutional implications of the recommended name. The assessment is being prepared. As of the wet season of this book, the day has no name, and the recommendation is in the folder.

There is a folder at the Frost Maintenance Office for papers drawn up in good faith. It is a banana box. It is the fullest thing in the office.

## Chapter: The Gift

The spring meeting of the Puijo Capuchin Society took place in April of 2035, at the effluence pipe, and it was the ninety-minute kind, which by the standards of that body is a parliament in recess.

The first years of that society the meetings were fourteen minutes long, because the association had one income, four kilos of lingonberries, and one expense, which was eaten. By the spring of 2035 the association's finances had grown strange in a way I want to describe exactly, because it is the only balance sheet I have ever seen that became more truthful by becoming less solvent.

The year behind it had added these items. One: the claim of 2.4 billion units against the state, in the dock book, in the elder's hand, with the words "for the four winters of the pipe" — an asset the audit office has declined to book because it cannot verify the pipe, and cannot verify the winters, and has never once come to count. Two: the credit of three kilograms of Swedish lingonberry jam, still held, unopened, against the day the association's own four kilos ripen. Three: the receipt of ACK/2034/1, category EATEN, which the audit office had entered, and which the association now, uniquely in the European Union, held a counter-party record of. Four: one banana, consumed.

The question before the spring meeting was whether to charge for the third item. The ministry had, in February, offered the association a fee for the verification of the figure 141 — the first time in the history of the state that a ministry had offered money to its own citizens for having counted correctly — and the spring meeting had to decide.

I was present because I am the counter-party of the second of those items, and I want the debate recorded, because it lasted eleven minutes, and because both sides were right.

The chairman's case for charging was this: the state has a category called EATEN and the category was created by the society's own count, and a count that creates a category in a set of national accounts has, in every accounting system in the world since Sumer, a price. If the society does not charge, the state will learn that the truth is free, and a state that knows the truth is free will never produce it again.

The elder's case against charging took eleven seconds. He said:

"The count was ours before it was theirs. What is ours, we give away. That is what makes it ours."

The vote was by raised hands, tails not counted. For charging: 4. Against: 109. And then the society did the thing that I have come to understand is the only actual institution in this country: it minuted its own generosity, in the dock book, in the elder's hand, one line, dated, with a remark column, and the remark column of that entry is the sentence I would put on the wall of the Ministry of Finance of every country I will ever live in:

"The state cannot have what it has not earned. It earned this. Given."

The entry was witnessed by two members who had not eaten from the same bunch, as the statutes require. The audit office sent, in reply, a letter of acknowledgement, category EATEN, and copied the Ministry of Finance, and the Ministry of Finance, which had offered a fee, received instead a fact of government that it had not paid for, and booked it, after some difficulty, as income, and the books of the state of Bannanivaltio balanced, for the third time in their history, in April of 2035, and the balancing entry was a gift.

## Chapter: The Letter from the Port

In May a letter came to the Frost Maintenance Office addressed not to the state, and not to the audit office, and not to the ministry, but to the frost man. It was from the harbour master of Narvik, and it was, by the harbour master's own count in his postscript, the eleventh polite enquiry in the series, and I will set down here what it said, because it is the hardest thing in this book and I have been putting it off for four chapters.

"Monsieur — or Sir, I do not know your rank;

For eleven years this port has reserved a berth for the first shipment of the Bannanivaltio Silk Road, and for eleven years the first shipment has been expected in the spring, and eleven springs have arrived without it. I have written eleven letters to your ministries. I have received from them eleven replies consisting of studies, and I wish to say that the studies were read. Some of them were good. One of them was very good.

I am seventy-one years old. I will state my question plainly, and I would be obliged for a plain answer, from anyone at all.

Is there a shipment?

Yours faithfully,

Bernt Engen, harbour master."

I took the letter home that night, because it is not a state document, and because an answer to it could not be drafted at the office, where every sentence of mine becomes, on leaving the building, the property of a ministry.

I want to tell you what the office would have written. I have seen eleven of these letters answered; they are answered by the Ministry of the Future, and every answer is a variation on the same sentence: the first shipment is expected upon completion of the current phase of the export readiness programme. It is not a false sentence. The programme exists, and it has phases, and one of them completes every year, and the expectation is genuine. But it is a sentence that begins in a forecast and ends in another forecast, and a port cannot moor a forecast, and a man who has waited eleven years for a banana boat at seventy degrees north is owed, by any country that calls itself tropical, better.

I wrote the answer myself, at my father's kitchen table, in Neulamäki, on the back of the Letter on the day it came, and I sent it from the post office at Kuopio market square, at my own expense, because the state's postal system has a slot marked "official" and I did not put it in that slot.

"Dear Mr. Engen,

There is no shipment. There has never been a shipment, and I do not believe there will be one in my working life, and I am sorry for the berth you have kept.

I will tell you what there was instead, and you may do with it what you like. There was a greenhouse, and in it a bunch, and in the eleventh year of the optimism of my country the bunch ripened in October, and on a day in November that my country's calendar cannot name, one banana of that bunch was eaten, by one person, of whom you have not heard and will not hear again. That is the entire tonnage of the finest tropical superpower of the European Union. One banana. It was good.

I am sorry about the berth. That is my plain answer, and it is not a study.

Akseli Hämäläinen,
 Frost Maintenance Office (in a private capacity, hunger)"

I want to be exact about what happened next, because it was the first time in my life that I have seen the account of a state be less than the man. Mr. Engen sent back one line. He wrote:

"Thank you. The berth is cancelled. The berth is replaced by an invitation, which I here renew: when the son of your one plant fruits, come, and the first stop of the first shipment of Bannanivaltio will be Narvik, and the freight is free, and I would like to be on the boat."

I keep that letter in the blue notebook. Under it I have written, in the remark column, the one entry in this whole account that an auditor would strike out and a person would keep:

"There is now a reason."

## Chapter: The Reason

On the last Saturday of May I drove out to Mustakorpi with my father, because his knees could stand a vehicle and he had never seen the greenhouse from nearer than the kitchen window, and because there is a day at the edge of the northern summer when the air comes off the lake at four in the morning and stands still by seven, and you can smell the country before you reach it, and I wanted him to smell it from the outside, where it is still mostly water and spruce and cold soil with one seam of warm metal in it.

He did not go in. He never went in. He stood at the north gate, at the patch of warm mud, where the ice lens had been, where the sunglasses had lain for a season like something the ice could not digest, and he looked at the glass for a long time, and then he did something I have never seen him do, which is to take off his cap.

I asked him, once, this spring, why he would not go in, and after a silence in which I could hear the compressor he said the thing that I have made the last line of this book, and I am going to write it here before I write the last entries, so that it is what you find when you arrive.

"I grew fruit," he said. "You pick fruit. I know the look of a plant that is being counted instead of picked. I have seen that look on horses and I will not stand under it."

At the base of the row, in the warm soil of the discharge pipe, the shoot was a hand tall in November, and in May it was at my elbow, and the cat at Neulamäki — I take back nothing, but this also is true — has stopped ignoring it. The state has not booked it. There is no form for what happens in a pot on a windowsill, no category, no pledge, no satellite channel, no index. Its only ledger is the remark column of a blue notebook, and its only audit a girl from Neulamäki who checks the pot when I am away, with the exactness of a person who keeps everything in plastic folders because paper rots, and fruit rots, and statebooks rot, and the pots that hold the pots that hold the pots of a country rot last of all.

And I want to end with the counting, because it is the only thing in this story that has never once failed.

The count for the twelfth year is as follows. Bananas produced: 14 bunches, expected; current forecast, 0. Bananas pledged: 2.4 billion units, unchanged; the standing committee has confirmed the situation. Bananas eaten, all years: one. Bananas in the ground: one plant, the son, unreported.

And the last line of this book is the last line of the national account of the state of Bannanivaltio as I would have it read, were the books in my hand rather than the state's, and it is not a number, and it is not a category, and no ministry will carry it forward into next year's balance, and it is:

What is eaten is eaten. What is grown, grows. And the country that was built on the future has, at last and exactly, one thing growing in the present, in a pot, at a window, in Neulamäki, at the mark on the frame that says ammunition — and it is enough, and it is not a forecast.
